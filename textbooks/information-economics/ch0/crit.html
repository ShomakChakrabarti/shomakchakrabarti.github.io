Testing and criticising a model#

Good criticism names an assumption#

“The model is too abstract” and “people are not that rational” are complaints, not criticisms. Every model is abstract, and neither complaint says what would change if the model were made less so. A useful criticism does two things.

  1. It names which assumption fails in the situation at hand.

  2. It says what changes in the conclusions when it does.

A criticism of that form can be answered: either the conclusion does not depend on the assumption, or a better model can be built without it. Much of the progress in economics, including every chapter of this book, has come from criticisms of exactly this kind.

Common mistake

It is easy to be an armchair critic of any model, in a seminar or in a pub. Opinions of the form “it’s more complicated than that” are always true and never useful. The test of a criticism is whether it tells you what to do next.

Example 3 (Criticising the model of the capital market)

[ch:fm] builds the standard model of saving and investment. One could object that people live for more than two periods; but the results extend to many periods (Generalisations), so this criticism changes nothing. One could object that tomorrow’s income and the returns to investment are uncertain; and this does change the conclusions. With a known future, two savings plans with the same average outcome are equally good, so the model cannot explain why people save more when their income becomes riskier, or why they prefer safe projects to risky ones with the same average return (What the model assumes about the future). The first criticism names an assumption that does not matter; the second names one that does, and repairing it is the subject of [ch:cu].

Testing predictions#

A model is tested by its predictions, and the most useful predictions are comparative statics: how an outcome changes when one thing changes and everything else is held fixed. Proposition 1 is an example: it predicts the size of the fall in smoking, and that the fall does not depend on income. Data can confirm or contradict a prediction like this. When the data contradict it, the habits of the toolkit apply: find the assumption responsible, and ask what a model without it predicts.

Each chapter of this book ends its main argument by taking the model to real markets. The theory meets the data, for example, shows that people buy insurance against small risks at prices that expected utility theory finds very hard to explain; the response is not to abandon the model but to identify which of its assumptions is failing.

Advanced

Why realistic assumptions matter for policy. A model whose assumptions are false can still predict well while circumstances stay as they were. Lucas (1976) pointed out that this is not enough for evaluating a new policy: if people’s behaviour adapts to the policy, a model fitted to past behaviour will mispredict, however well it fitted. A model whose assumptions describe how people decide, rather than how they happened to behave, is the one to trust when the rules change. This is a reason to care about assumptions that Friedman’s view underrates.
