Introduction#

A tax on cigarettes#

The government wants to know whether a higher price will reduce smoking, and commissions a survey. Consider two questions it might ask a surgeon who smokes.

  • If cigarettes cost 20% more next year, will you smoke more or less?

  • Suppose your income and your workload next year are the same as this year. If cigarettes cost 20% more, will you smoke more or less?

Before reading on, decide which question you would put in the survey.

To question A she answers “more”: she has a run of difficult operations coming up, and she smokes when she is stressed. That answer is honest, but it mixes three things: the price, her workload and her income. If the survey reports answers to question A, it will conclude that the tax does nothing, or even that it backfires. Question B holds workload and income fixed, so its answer isolates the one factor the policy is about.

Question B is a model. It does not deny that stress and income matter. It shuts them down because they are not what this question is about. Every model in this book works the same way, and this chapter is about how such models are built, what they are for, and how to tell a good one from a bad one.

What you will learn#

After working through this chapter you should be able to:

  1. explain what an economic model is, and what it means for a model to isolate a mechanism;

  2. describe the five steps by which every model in this book is built, and identify them in a model you have not seen before;

  3. explain the trade-off between tractability and realism, and why several models of the same market can coexist;

  4. criticise a model usefully, by naming the assumption that fails and saying what changes when it does;

  5. read the results of a model (definitions, assumptions, lemmas, propositions, theorems) and know what a proof of each must show.

What you need. Nothing beyond school mathematics. One example uses a utility function and a derivative.

Roadmap. What a model is says what a model is. How the models in this book are built sets out the five steps and works through them for the cigarette tax. Simplicity, realism and competing models discusses simplicity, realism and competing models. Testing and criticising a model shows how to test and criticise a model. How to read the results of a model explains how to read the results and proofs in the rest of the book.

Proof toolkit: three habits used in every chapter

  • (T1) Hold fixed what the question is not about. Decide first which factor you want the effect of; everything else is held fixed by assumption.

  • (T2) Find the assumption doing the work. Every result rests on a few assumptions that matter and many that do not. Know which are which.

  • (T3) Ask what changes when it fails. Relax the key assumption and see which conclusions survive. That is both how models are criticised and how the next model is built.
