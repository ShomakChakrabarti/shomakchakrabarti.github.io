How the models in this book are built#

Five steps#

Every chapter follows the same five steps.

  1. A question. It should be specific enough to have an answer. For example: does hidden quality destroy a market ([ch:ad])? Does a higher interest rate make people save more ([ch:fm])?

  2. Assumptions. Decide what to hold fixed, and state it precisely. Some assumptions describe the environment (two periods, two types of worker); some, called axioms, describe how people choose, such as the axioms of expected utility in [ch:cu].

  3. Preferences. Say what each person wants: a utility function, and later an expected utility function. People are assumed to choose the best option they can afford, given what they know.

  4. Equilibrium. Find what everyone does, given what everyone else does. With one decision maker this is just her best choice; with many, it is a situation in which no one wants to change their choice, such as a price at which buyers and sellers of used cars both trade willingly ([ch:ad]).

  5. Results. State what follows exactly, as lemmas, propositions and theorems, and prove them. Then ask what the results predict, and whether the predictions hold.

The five steps for the cigarette tax#

Here is question B, written as a model.

Example 1 (A model of the cigarette tax)

Question. By how much does a 20% rise in the price of cigarettes reduce the number the surgeon smokes?

Assumptions. She has a weekly income \(m\) to spend on cigarettes \(x\), at price \(p\) per pack, and on everything else \(y\), at price \(1\). Her workload, and so her stress, is fixed; it is summarised by a number \(a>0\) that measures how much she values smoking. Her income is fixed at \(m\ge a\).

Preferences. Her utility is \(u(x,y)=a\ln x+y\), and she chooses \((x,y)\) to maximise it subject to the budget \(px+y=m\).

Equilibrium. There is one decision maker, so equilibrium is her best choice. Substituting \(y=m-px\), she maximises \(a\ln x+m-px\). The derivative is \(a/x-p\), which is positive for small \(x\) and negative for large \(x\), so the best choice sets it to zero:

\[ x^*(p)=\frac{a}{p},\qquad y^*=m-a . \]

Result. See Proposition 1.

Proposition 1 (Demand for cigarettes)

In Example 1, a rise in the price from \(p\) to \(1.2p\) lowers the number of cigarettes smoked from \(a/p\) to \(a/(1.2p)\), a fall of one sixth (about 17%), whatever her income and whatever the value of \(a\).

Proof of Proposition 1

By Example 1, \(x^*(1.2p)/x^*(p)=(a/1.2p)/(a/p)=1/1.2=5/6\), so consumption falls by \(1-5/6=1/6\). Neither \(m\) nor \(a\) appears in the ratio.

Now put the surgeon’s stress back in. Suppose \(a=10\) and \(p=5\), so she smokes \(x^*=2\) packs a week. Next year the price rises to \(6\), and her operations raise \(a\) to \(14\). She then smokes \(14/6\approx2.3\) packs. An analyst who compares this year with next, as question A does, sees consumption rise after the tax and concludes that the tax failed. The model separates the two effects: the price alone takes her from \(2\) to \(10/6\approx1.7\) packs, and the stress alone from \(1.7\) to \(2.3\).

Two points are worth noticing. First, the model made a prediction that could be wrong: a fall of exactly one sixth. Second, part of that prediction comes from the form of utility chosen. With \(u=a\ln x+y\), extra income is spent entirely on other goods, so income has no effect on smoking; the model has shut down the income channel by its choice of preferences, not only by its stated assumptions. Knowing which assumption produces which conclusion is habit (T2), and it is the most important skill this book tries to teach.
