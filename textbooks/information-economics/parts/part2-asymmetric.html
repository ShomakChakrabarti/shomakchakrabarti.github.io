Introduction to asymmetric information#

When one side knows more#

In the competitive markets of introductory economics, everyone knows what is being traded and on what terms. Many of the most important transactions are not like that. An employer cannot see how hard a manager works. An insurer does not know how carefully a driver drives, or how healthy a new customer is. A buyer of a used car knows less about it than its owner. A firm hiring a graduate cannot observe her ability, only her degree.

Definition (Asymmetric information)

A situation of asymmetric information is one in which some party to a transaction has relevant information that another party does not, and cannot costlessly obtain or verify.

The informed party will use its information in its own interest, and the uninformed party knows this. So the uninformed party must design contracts, set prices or form beliefs that take account of what the other side knows and how it will behave. The result is rarely the outcome that would arise with full information. Mutually beneficial trades may not happen, contracts may expose people to risk they would rather avoid, and resources may be spent on activities whose only purpose is to convey information. This part asks when those losses arise, how large they are, and what can be done about them.

Two kinds of asymmetry#

The chapters of this part are organised around a single distinction: whether the hidden thing is an action taken after the parties have agreed to trade, or a characteristic that exists before.

The two kinds of asymmetric information, by when the asymmetry arises relative to the agreement to trade.

Hidden action (moral hazard). Information is symmetric when the contract is signed, but afterwards one party takes an action that affects the other and cannot be observed or contracted on. The term comes from insurance, where cover can make the insured less careful. The uninformed party’s problem is incentives: it can only reward what it can see, such as output, and rewarding output exposes the informed party to risk.

Hidden information. One party knows a characteristic relevant to the trade before it takes place. The uninformed party’s problem is selection: whoever chooses to trade at given terms is not a random draw. Two responses are studied in this book. If the informed side cannot prove what it knows, the market may unravel (adverse selection). If it can take a costly action that is cheaper for good types, it may reveal its type by taking it (signalling). A third response, in which the uninformed side offers a menu of contracts designed so that different types choose differently (screening), builds on the same tools.

The order runs from the simplest strategic setting to the richest. In Chapter 3 the information is symmetric when the contract is signed, so the principal can design the whole relationship; the difficulty is only that she cannot see what the agent does. In Chapter 4 nobody designs anything: a price forms in a market, and the informed side responds to it. In Chapter 5 the informed side acts strategically to reveal what it knows, and beliefs about actions that are never taken shape what happens.

Common threads#

Although the three chapters study different problems, the same ideas recur.

  • Constraints from the informed side’s incentives. In each model the uninformed party must respect a constraint that would not exist with full information: an incentive constraint for the agent in Chapter 3, the sellers’ decision to sell in Chapter 4, the low type’s decision not to mimic in Chapter 5. Writing that constraint down is always the first step of the analysis.

  • First best and second best. Each chapter compares the outcome with full information (the first best) with the best that can be achieved given what can be observed (the second best). The gap between them is the cost of asymmetric information.

  • Bayes’ rule and beliefs. Prices, wages and contracts depend on what the uninformed side believes, and those beliefs must be consistent with how the informed side behaves.

  • Remedies and their limits. Verification, reputation, warranties, mandates and the design of contracts all reduce the cost; each chapter asks which of them the evidence shows at work.

A short history#

The economics of asymmetric information took shape in the 1960s and 1970s. Kenneth Arrow (1963) brought moral hazard and adverse selection from the language of insurance into economics in a study of medical care. George Akerlof’s “The Market for ‘Lemons’ ” (1970), Michael Spence’s job-market signalling (1973) and Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz’s analysis of insurance markets (1976) followed; Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information. James Mirrlees and William Vickrey shared the 1996 prize for the theory of incentives under asymmetric information, and Bengt Holmström and Oliver Hart the 2016 prize for contract theory, to which Chapter 3 is an introduction.

How to read this part#

The three chapters can be read in order or independently. Each starts with an introduction and a model, solves benchmarks with symmetric information, and then proves the main results with the asymmetry, before turning to case studies, generalisations for research students, and exercises. Chapter 3 uses the insurance results and the Jensen gap from Chapter 2 most heavily; Chapters 4 and 5 use Bayes’ rule and expected values.
