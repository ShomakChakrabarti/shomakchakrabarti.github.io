Introduction to choice over time#

Markets that need tomorrow#

A labour market can clear in an afternoon: a worker sells an hour, a firm pays for it, and both go home. A capital market cannot. A household that saves gives up consumption today in exchange for a promise of more tomorrow; a firm that builds a factory pays now for output it will sell for years. Both sides of the market are choosing plans, and every plan reaches into the future.

The economics of such choices was set out by Irving Fisher (1930), who showed that a single price, the interest rate, connects the two sides. Savers compare the interest rate with how much they would rather consume now than later. Investors compare it with the return their projects earn. When the market clears, the interest rate makes the two plans add up.

What this part does#

Chapter 1 begins with the question every chapter in this book must answer first: what is a model for, and how do we judge one? It then builds the two-period model of saving and the net present value rule for investment, using nothing beyond consumer theory: budget lines, indifference curves, tangency, and the Slutsky decomposition.

Why this part comes first#

The model of Chapter 1 works only because it assumes the future is known: tomorrow’s income, the interest rate, every cash flow of a project. It is a good model of time and a poor model of risk. What the model assumes about the future shows exactly where it breaks: two plans with the same average outcome are treated as equal, although almost nobody would choose them indifferently.

  • Chapter 2 supplies what is missing: a way to rank lotteries (expected utility) and a measure of how much a person dislikes risk.

  • Chapter 3 asks what happens when the saver lends to someone whose effort she cannot see, and Chapters 4 and 5 when she cannot see the borrower’s type.

How to read this part#

Part I consists of Chapter 1. Readers who have met intertemporal choice before can skim The two-period model and The best plan, but should read A change in the interest rate (the Slutsky decomposition for lenders and borrowers) and What the model assumes about the future, which leads into the rest of the book.
