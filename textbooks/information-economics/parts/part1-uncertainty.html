Introduction to choice under uncertainty#

Decisions whose outcomes are not yours to choose#

Almost every economic decision is taken before the facts are in. A farmer plants before the weather is known. A firm hires before it knows how good the worker is. A student chooses a degree before knowing which jobs will exist when she graduates. In each case the decision maker chooses an action, but the outcome depends also on a state of the world that she does not control and does not know.

Economics before the twentieth century had little to say about such choices beyond “maximise the expected payoff”. Daniel Bernoulli showed in 1738 why that rule cannot be the whole story: a gamble that pays £2 if the first head comes on the first toss, £4 if on the second, £8 if on the third, and so on, has an infinite expected value, yet nobody would pay more than a few pounds to play it (the St Petersburg paradox). Bernoulli’s answer was that people care about the utility of money, and that each extra pound is worth less than the one before. Two centuries later von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944) turned the idea into a theory: if a person’s choices among risky options obey a few consistency rules, she behaves as if she maximises the expected value of a utility function. That theory, expected utility, is the foundation of this part and of everything that follows.

Risk and uncertainty#

Frank Knight (1921) distinguished risk, where the probabilities of the possible outcomes are known (a fair die, an insurance pool with well-understood claim rates), from uncertainty, where they are not (a new technology, a pandemic). This book works throughout with risk in Knight’s sense, and follows modern usage in calling it “choice under uncertainty”. The probabilities may be objective, like those of a die, or subjective, the decision maker’s own beliefs; what matters is that she can state them and that she updates them consistently, by Bayes’ rule, when she learns something new.

Three questions this part answers#

  1. How should we describe a risky choice? As a choice among lotteries: probability distributions over outcomes. The decision maker ranks the lotteries, not the outcomes.

  2. When can that ranking be summarised by expected utility? Exactly when it satisfies four axioms: completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence. The utility function is then unique up to positive affine transformations, so differences in utility have meaning.

  3. What does the shape of the utility function tell us? Its curvature measures attitude to risk. Concavity is risk aversion; the Arrow–Pratt coefficients measure how much; and risk aversion explains why people buy insurance, and how much of it.

Where this part sits#

Part I treated the future as known. Its last section (What the model assumes about the future) showed what goes wrong once tomorrow is a gamble: plans with the same average are ranked equally, though few people would be indifferent between them. This part repairs that, and in doing so supplies the tools for everything after it.

Part III studies markets and contracts in which one side knows something the other does not. Every one of its models is built from the tools of this part.

  • In Chapter 3 (moral hazard), a risk-averse agent’s dislike of risk is what makes incentives costly: the principal must pay a risk premium to make the agent’s pay depend on output. The certainty equivalent and Jensen’s inequality from Chapter 2 measure that cost.

  • In Chapters 4 and 5 (adverse selection and signalling), buyers and employers form beliefs about quality they cannot see. Bayes’ rule from Chapter 2 tells them what to believe after seeing who sells, or which credential a worker holds.

  • Throughout, efficiency is judged in expected-utility terms, and insurance, the last model of Chapter 2, is the benchmark against which every contract in Part III is compared.

How to read this part#

Part II consists of Chapter 2. Readers who have met expected utility before can skim The model and Expected utility and start at Attitudes towards risk (attitudes to risk) and The demand for insurance (insurance), which Part III uses most. The case studies in The theory meets the data show where the theory describes behaviour well and where it does not; they are worth reading before Part III, which relies on expected utility for decisions involving large stakes, where it works best.
