2.4. Attitudes towards risk#

2.4.1. Certainty equivalent and risk premium#

Definition 2.4 (Certainty equivalent, risk premium)

Let \(u\) be continuous and strictly increasing. The certainty equivalent of lottery \(\pi\) is the amount \(c_\pi\) with

\[ u(c_\pi)=\sum_{x\in X}\pi_x\,u(x),\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad c_\pi=u^{-1}\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\pi_x\,u(x)\Big). \]

The risk premium is \(\rho_\pi\coloneqq\E[\pi]-c_\pi\).

Interpretation. The certainty equivalent is the sure amount that is exactly as good as the lottery. If you own the lottery, it is the smallest price at which you would sell it; if you face the lottery as a risk, \(\E[\pi]-c_\pi\) is the most you would pay, on top of its expected cost, to be rid of it. In Example 2.2, \(c_\pi=81\) and the risk premium is \(3\).

2.4.2. Risk aversion#

Definition 2.5 (Risk aversion, neutrality, loving)

A decision maker is risk averse if for every non-degenerate lottery \(\pi\) she strictly prefers the sure amount \(\E[\pi]\): \(\delta_{\E[\pi]}\succ\pi\). She is risk neutral if \(\delta_{\E[\pi]}\sim\pi\) for all \(\pi\), and risk loving if \(\pi\succ\delta_{\E[\pi]}\) for every non-degenerate \(\pi\).

Equivalently, she is risk averse if and only if \(c_\pi<\E[\pi]\) (a positive risk premium) for every non-degenerate lottery.

Proposition 2.3 (Risk aversion is concavity)

Let the outcome set be an interval and \(u\) continuous and strictly increasing. The decision maker is risk averse if and only if \(u\) is strictly concave. She is risk neutral if and only if \(u\) is affine, and risk loving if and only if \(u\) is strictly convex.

Proof strategy (Proposition 2.3)

Both directions are Jensen’s inequality (T2). The only care needed is in “only if”: risk aversion is a statement about all lotteries, while strict concavity is a statement about two points. A two-outcome lottery is exactly a pair of points with weights, so applying risk aversion to two-outcome lotteries gives the definition of strict concavity directly.

Proof of Proposition 2.3

If. Let \(u\) be strictly concave and \(\pi\) non-degenerate. Jensen’s inequality for strictly concave functions gives \(\sum_x\pi_xu(x)<u\big(\sum_x\pi_xx\big)=u(\E[\pi])\). Since expected utility represents \(\succsim\), \(\delta_{\E[\pi]}\succ\pi\).

Only if. Suppose the decision maker is risk averse. Take any \(y<z\) and \(t\in(0,1)\), and the lottery \(\pi=\{y\circ t;\ z\circ(1-t)\}\), which is non-degenerate with mean \(ty+(1-t)z\). Risk aversion gives \(\delta_{ty+(1-t)z}\succ\pi\), that is,

\[ u\big(ty+(1-t)z\big)>t\,u(y)+(1-t)\,u(z). \]

This is the definition of strict concavity.

Why this step?

The lecture proof says “\(u\) satisfies Jensen’s inequality, so it is concave”. That is right, but the reason is that Jensen’s inequality for two-point lotteries is the definition of concavity; nothing deeper is needed.

Neutral and loving. Replace \(>\) by \(=\) or \(<\) throughout.

Interpretation. Concavity means diminishing marginal utility of money: the hundredth pound adds less than the first. A fair gamble of \(\pm h\) then loses more utility on the downside than it gains on the upside, so the decision maker would rather not take it. Risk aversion is not a separate psychological trait added to the model; within expected utility it is diminishing marginal utility.

2.4.3. Measuring risk aversion#

A natural first idea is to measure risk aversion by \(-u''\): a more concave function, a more risk-averse person. This fails, because \(u\) is only unique up to \(au+b\) (Proposition 2.2). The functions \(u(x)=\ln x\) and \(v(x)=3\ln x\) describe the same person, yet \(v''(x)=-3/x^2<-1/x^2=u''(x)\). Dividing by \(u'\) removes the scale.

Definition 2.6 (Arrow–Pratt measures)

For twice-differentiable \(u\) with \(u'>0\), the coefficient of absolute risk aversion at \(x\) is

\[ \lambda(x)\coloneqq-\frac{u''(x)}{u'(x)}, \]

and the coefficient of relative risk aversion is \(\mu(x)\coloneqq x\,\lambda(x)=-x\,u''(x)/u'(x)\).

Proposition 2.4 (Properties of the Arrow–Pratt measures)

  • (a) \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are unchanged when \(u\) is replaced by \(au+b\) with \(a>0\).

  • (b) If \(\lambda(x)>0\) for all \(x\), the decision maker is risk averse.

  • (c) The risk premium of a small risk is approximately proportional to \(\lambda\): for a lottery with mean \(\bar x\) and small variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\rho\approx\tfrac12\lambda(\bar x)\,\sigma^2\) (Proposition 2.8).

Proof of Proposition 2.4

(a) \((au+b)'=au'\) and \((au+b)''=au''\), so the factor \(a\) cancels in \(-u''/u'\). (b) \(\lambda>0\) and \(u'>0\) give \(u''<0\), so \(u\) is strictly concave and Proposition 2.3 applies. (c) is proved in Generalisations.

Why this step?

In (b) the converse fails at isolated points: a strictly concave \(u\) can have \(u''=0\) at a single point. So “risk averse iff \(\lambda>0\)”, as on the slides, should read “\(\lambda>0\) everywhere implies risk averse”.

Absolute or relative? \(\lambda\) measures aversion to risks of a given size in pounds; \(\mu\) measures aversion to risks of a given size relative to wealth. Whether a richer person minds a £100 gamble less (decreasing absolute risk aversion) is an empirical question, and most evidence says yes. Whether she minds a 10% gamble less is less clear; constant relative risk aversion is the usual benchmark.

2.4.4. Interpreting risk attitudes#

  1. Risk aversion is about outcomes, not probabilities. In expected utility all attitudes to risk come from the shape of \(u\). The decision maker uses the true probabilities; she does not overweight unlikely disasters. The theory meets the data shows this is a real restriction.

  2. Small risks are almost free to bear. By Proposition 2.4(c) the risk premium is proportional to the variance, which for a risk of size \(h\) is of order \(h^2\). So for small risks a risk-averse person behaves almost like a risk-neutral one: she will pay little above the expected loss to insure them. Remember this when we look at deductibles.

  3. Risk aversion is a reason to trade. If two people face independent risks, or one is less risk averse, both can gain by moving risk to whoever minds it less. Insurance companies, which pool many independent risks, are the clearest example, and the next section studies what they offer.
