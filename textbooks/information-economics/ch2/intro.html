2.1. Introduction#

2.1.1. An umbrella, a phone and a pension#

You are deciding whether to take an umbrella. If it rains you will be glad of it; if it does not, you will carry it around all day for nothing. You do not know which will happen, but the forecast says there is a 30% chance of rain. Whatever you decide, you are not choosing an outcome. You are choosing between two situations, each of which leads to different outcomes with different probabilities.

At the checkout of a phone shop you are offered insurance against damage for £8 a month. The phone costs £900. You would rather not pay, but you would also rather not lose £900. Whether the insurance is worth buying depends on how likely you are to break the phone, on the price, and on how much you mind a large loss compared with a stream of small payments.

Your employer asks how you want your pension invested: a “cautious” fund whose value grows slowly and steadily, or a “growth” fund that grows faster on average but can fall sharply in a bad year. Two people with the same information and the same salary may sensibly choose differently.

In all three cases:

  • the decision maker does not control, and does not know, which outcome will occur;

  • she can describe each option by the outcomes it can lead to and their probabilities;

  • her choice depends both on those probabilities and on her attitude to risk: how much she minds the spread of outcomes, not just their average.

2.1.2. Why this chapter comes next#

Chapter 1 valued saving plans and investment projects as if the future were known, and ended with two puzzles it could not solve: why a saver puts aside more when tomorrow’s income becomes riskier, though its average is unchanged (Example 1.6), and why investors are not indifferent between two projects with the same expected net present value (Example 1.7). This chapter supplies what was missing: a way to rank lotteries, and a measure of how much a person dislikes risk. With it, a cash flow becomes what it really is, a lottery paid over time.

Every model in the rest of the book has someone choosing under uncertainty. In Chapter 3 an agent is paid according to an output that depends on chance as well as on his effort, and the cost of incentives comes entirely from his dislike of risk; in Chapter 4 a buyer pays for a car of unknown quality; in Chapter 5 a firm pays a worker of unknown productivity. This chapter supplies the tools all of those chapters use: expected utility, the shape of the utility function as a description of risk attitude, certainty equivalents and risk premia, and the logic of insurance.

2.1.3. The central idea: utility is curved#

If you only cared about the average outcome, you would be indifferent between £50 for sure and a coin flip between £0 and £100. Most people are not: they prefer the sure £50, and would accept rather less than £50 for sure in place of the coin flip. The theory of this chapter explains this with a single object, a utility function \(u\) over money, and a single rule, maximise the expected value of \(u\). If \(u\) is concave, each extra pound adds less utility than the one before, losses hurt more than equal gains help, and the decision maker dislikes risk. Much of the chapter is about making this precise: why the expected-utility rule follows from a few plausible axioms, what the curvature of \(u\) measures, and what it implies for the demand for insurance.

2.1.4. What you will learn#

After working through this chapter you should be able to:

  1. describe risky options as lotteries and preferences over them, and state the axioms of expected utility and what each one rules out;

  2. prove the expected utility theorem for a finite set of outcomes, and show that the utility function is unique up to positive affine transformations;

  3. compute certainty equivalents and risk premia, and prove that risk aversion is equivalent to a strictly concave utility function;

  4. compute and interpret the Arrow–Pratt measures of absolute and relative risk aversion;

  5. derive the demand for insurance: full insurance at actuarially fair prices and partial insurance (a deductible) at loaded prices;

  6. judge the theory against two pieces of evidence, the Allais paradox and observed deductible choices;

  7. (Advanced) compare risk aversion across people (Pratt’s theorem), approximate the risk premium, rank lotteries by riskiness, and state Rabin’s calibration critique.

What you need. Probability distributions with finitely many outcomes, expected values, and single-variable calculus (derivatives, concavity, first-order conditions).

Roadmap. The model sets out lotteries, preferences and the axioms. Expected utility proves the expected utility theorem and studies what the utility function does and does not tell us. Attitudes towards risk defines risk aversion and measures it. The demand for insurance derives the demand for insurance. The theory meets the data takes the theory to the Allais paradox and to deductible choices. Generalisations generalises for research students. Exercises and corrections close the chapter.

Proof toolkit: four techniques used throughout this chapter

  • (T1) Mixing. A mixture \(w\pi+(1-w)\rho\) of two lotteries is the lottery “play \(\pi\) with probability \(w\), \(\rho\) otherwise”. Writing a lottery as a mixture of simpler ones, and using that expected utility is linear in probabilities, drives the expected utility theorem and the Allais argument.

  • (T2) Jensen’s inequality. If \(u\) is strictly concave and \(X\) is a non-degenerate random variable, \(\E[u(X)]<u(\E[X])\). For two outcomes this is the definition of strict concavity. It is the mathematical content of “risk aversion”.

  • (T3) Sign the derivative. To show a choice is optimal, sign the derivative of the objective on either side of it instead of only solving the first-order condition. This handles corners and proves uniqueness in one step.

  • (T4) Second-order Taylor approximation. For small risks, \(u(\bar x+\varepsilon)\approx u(\bar x)+u'(\bar x)\varepsilon +\tfrac12u''(\bar x)\varepsilon^2\). This turns the curvature of \(u\) into a price of risk.
