2.2. The model#

2.2.1. The model in one paragraph#

A single decision maker faces a set of possible outcomes, such as amounts of money. She does not choose an outcome; she chooses among lotteries, probability distributions over outcomes. She has preferences over lotteries, and we ask which preferences can be described by a simple formula: assign a number \(u(x)\) to each outcome and rank lotteries by the expected value of that number.

2.2.2. Outcomes and lotteries#

Let \(X\subseteq\R_+\) be the set of possible outcomes: final wealth, profit, winnings. Until Generalisations we assume \(X\) is finite, which avoids measure-theoretic detail without losing any of the ideas.

Definition 2.1 (Lottery)

A (simple) lottery is a probability distribution \(\pi\) on \(X\): a number \(\pi_x\ge0\) for each \(x\in X\), with \(\sum_{x\in X}\pi_x=1\). We write \(\Pi_X\) for the set of all lotteries on \(X\), and

\[ \pi=\{x_1\circ\pi_1;\ x_2\circ\pi_2;\ \dots;\ x_n\circ\pi_n\} \]

for the lottery that gives \(x_i\) with probability \(\pi_i\). The degenerate lottery \(\delta_x\) gives \(x\) for sure.

Definition 2.2 (Mixture)

For lotteries \(\pi,\rho\) and \(w\in[0,1]\), the mixture \(w\pi+(1-w)\rho\) is the lottery that gives \(x\) with probability \(w\pi_x+(1-w)\rho_x\).

Interpretation. A mixture is a two-stage lottery: first a coin with probability \(w\) of heads is tossed; on heads you play \(\pi\), on tails you play \(\rho\). Multiplying out the probabilities gives a one-stage lottery, and we assume the decision maker cares only about that final distribution. This is sometimes called reduction of compound lotteries, and it is already built into Definition 2.2.

2.2.3. Preferences over lotteries, not outcomes#

The decision maker has a preference relation \(\succsim\) on \(\Pi_X\). We write \(\pi\succsim\rho\) for “she finds \(\pi\) at least as good as \(\rho\)”, \(\pi\succ\rho\) for strict preference and \(\pi\sim\rho\) for indifference.

The objects being compared are situations, not outcomes. Preferring \(\pi\) to \(\rho\) does not mean that the outcome of \(\pi\) will be better; it can turn out worse. A good decision can have a bad outcome.

Example 2.1 (The weather)

Tomorrow is sunny (\(x=1\)) or rainy (\(x=0\)), and you prefer sun. Compare

\[\begin{split} \begin{align*} \pi&=\{1\circ0.4;\ 0\circ0.6\}\quad\text{(60\% chance of rain)},\\ \rho&=\{1\circ0.8;\ 0\circ0.2\}\quad\text{(20\% chance of rain)}. \end{align*} \end{split}\]

Since you prefer sun, any sensible preference ranks \(\rho\succ\pi\): it gives the better outcome with higher probability. Yet if you choose the trip with forecast \(\rho\), it may still rain. The preference is over the forecasts, not the weather.

2.2.4. Axioms#

Without restrictions, a preference relation over lotteries can rationalise any behaviour and so predicts nothing. We impose four axioms that we expect a consistent decision maker to satisfy.

Assumption 2.1 (Completeness)

For all \(\pi,\rho\in\Pi_X\): \(\pi\succsim\rho\) or \(\rho\succsim\pi\) (or both).

Assumption 2.2 (Transitivity)

For all \(\pi,\rho,\varphi\): if \(\pi\succsim\rho\) and \(\rho\succsim\varphi\), then \(\pi\succsim\varphi\).

Assumption 2.3 (Continuity)

For all \(\pi,\rho,\varphi\) with \(\pi\succ\rho\succ\varphi\), there is \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) with \(\rho\sim\alpha\pi+(1-\alpha)\varphi\).

Assumption 2.4 (Independence)

For all \(\pi,\rho,\varphi\) and all \(w\in(0,1]\):

\[ \pi\succsim\rho\iff w\pi+(1-w)\varphi\ \succsim\ w\rho+(1-w)\varphi . \]

What each axiom says.

  • Completeness and transitivity are the same as in consumer theory: every pair of options can be ranked, and the ranking has no cycles. A decision maker with cyclic preferences can be turned into a “money pump”, paying a little to swap \(\rho\) for \(\pi\), \(\varphi\) for \(\rho\), and \(\pi\) for \(\varphi\), round and round.

  • Continuity rules out lexicographic attitudes. If \(\pi\) is £100, \(\rho\) is £10 and \(\varphi\) is death, continuity says some small probability of death is acceptable in exchange for the extra £90. People do accept such risks (crossing a road to reach a cheaper shop), so the axiom is not as odd as it sounds.

  • Independence is the axiom specific to uncertainty. If you prefer \(\pi\) to \(\rho\), then you should still prefer it when both are played only with probability \(w\), and with probability \(1-w\) the same lottery \(\varphi\) is played instead. In the \(1-w\) event the two options give the same thing, so that event should not matter. It is the strongest and most disputed axiom; The theory meets the data shows how people violate it.

Common mistake

Independence is not implied by completeness and transitivity, and it has no counterpart in consumer theory. Bundles of goods are consumed together, so complementarities are natural; the outcomes of a lottery are mutually exclusive, so what happens in one event cannot complement what happens in another. That is the intuitive case for independence, and also why its failures are interesting.

2.2.5. What we want to learn#

Definition 2.3 (Representation)

A function \(U:\Pi_X\to\R\) represents \(\succsim\) if for all \(\pi,\rho\): \(\pi\succsim\rho\iff U(\pi)\ge U(\rho)\).

Our question: is there a function \(u:X\to\R\) such that the expected utility

\[ U(\pi)=\sum_{x\in X}\pi_x\,u(x) \]

represents \(\succsim\)? If so, what does \(u\) tell us, and how unique is it?

2.2.6. A running example#

Example 2.2 (Insuring a phone)

Your wealth is \(100\) (in tens of pounds, say). With probability \(0.25\) you lose \(64\) of it; with probability \(0.75\) you lose nothing. So you face the lottery

\[ \pi=\{100\circ0.75;\ 36\circ0.25\},\qquad \E[\pi]=84 . \]

We will suppose you evaluate wealth with \(u(x)=\sqrt x\). Then your expected utility is \(0.75\cdot10+0.25\cdot6=9\), which is the utility of \(81\) for sure. So the lottery is worth the same to you as \(81\) for sure, \(3\) less than its average. That gap of \(3\) is the most you would pay, above the average loss, to be rid of the risk. The rest of the chapter explains each of these numbers.
