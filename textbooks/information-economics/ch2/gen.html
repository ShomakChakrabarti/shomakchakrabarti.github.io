2.7. Generalisations#

Advanced: for research students

This section compares risk aversion across people, proves the small-risk approximation, ranks lotteries by riskiness, and states Rabin’s calibration theorem. Throughout, outcomes lie in an interval \(I\) and \(u\) is twice continuously differentiable with \(u'>0\).

2.7.1. Comparing risk aversion: Pratt’s theorem#

Theorem 2.2 (Pratt 1964)

For two utilities \(u\) and \(v\), the following are equivalent:

  • (i) \(\lambda_v(x)\ge\lambda_u(x)\) for all \(x\in I\);

  • (ii) \(v=g\circ u\) for some increasing concave function \(g\);

  • (iii) \(c^v_\pi\le c^u_\pi\) for every lottery \(\pi\): \(v\)’s certainty equivalents are never above \(u\)’s.

Proof of Theorem 2.2

(i)\(\iff\)(ii). Let \(g=v\circ u^{-1}\), which is increasing. Then \(v'(x)=g'(u(x))u'(x)\) and \(v''(x)=g''(u(x))u'(x)^2+g'(u(x))u''(x)\). Dividing by \(-v'(x)\),

\[ \lambda_v(x)=\lambda_u(x)-\frac{g''(u(x))}{g'(u(x))}\,u'(x). \]

So \(\lambda_v\ge\lambda_u\) everywhere iff \(g''\le0\) everywhere.

(ii)\(\Rightarrow\)(iii). By Jensen’s inequality for concave \(g\), \(v(c^v_\pi)=\E[g(u(X))]\le g(\E[u(X)])=g(u(c^u_\pi))=v(c^u_\pi)\), and \(v\) is increasing.

(iii)\(\Rightarrow\)(ii). If \(g\) were not concave, there would be \(a<b\) in \(u(I)\) and \(t\in(0,1)\) with \(g(ta+(1-t)b)<tg(a)+(1-t)g(b)\). The lottery giving \(u^{-1}(a)\) with probability \(t\) and \(u^{-1}(b)\) otherwise has \(u(c^u)=ta+(1-t)b\) and \(v(c^v)=tg(a)+(1-t)g(b)>g(ta+(1-t)b)=v(c^u)\), so \(c^v>c^u\), contradicting (iii).

Why this step?

(ii) is the cleanest way to think about “more risk averse”: \(v\) is \(u\) followed by a further concave distortion. Statement (i) is the local test; (iii) is the behavioural meaning.

2.7.2. The small-risk approximation#

Proposition 2.8 (Arrow–Pratt approximation)

Let \(X=\bar x+\varepsilon\) with \(\E[\varepsilon]=0\) and \(\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2\). Then the risk premium satisfies

\[ \rho=\tfrac12\lambda(\bar x)\,\sigma^2+o(\sigma^2)\quad\text{as the risk shrinks.} \]

Proof of Proposition 2.8

By definition \(u(\bar x-\rho)=\E[u(\bar x+\varepsilon)]\). Expand both sides around \(\bar x\) (T4): the left is \(u(\bar x)-\rho u'(\bar x)+O(\rho^2)\); the right is \(u(\bar x)+\tfrac12u''(\bar x)\sigma^2+o(\sigma^2)\), because the first-order term has mean zero. Since \(\rho=O(\sigma^2)\), \(\rho^2=o(\sigma^2)\); equating and dividing by \(u'(\bar x)\) gives the result.

In Example 2.2, \(\lambda(84)=1/168\) and \(\sigma^2=768\), so the approximation gives \(2.3\) against the exact \(3\). The risk there is not small, so the approximation is only rough.

2.7.3. Ranking lotteries by risk#

Variance is not in general the right measure of risk for an expected-utility maximiser. The right ordering is the following.

Definition 2.8 (Mean-preserving spread)

Lottery \(\rho\) is a mean-preserving spread of \(\pi\) if a random variable with distribution \(\rho\) can be written as \(X+Z\), where \(X\) has distribution \(\pi\) and \(\E[Z\mid X]=0\).

Proposition 2.9 (Rothschild and Stiglitz 1970)

If \(\rho\) is a mean-preserving spread of \(\pi\), every risk-averse expected utility maximiser weakly prefers \(\pi\) to \(\rho\). (Conversely, if every concave \(u\) weakly prefers \(\pi\), then \(\rho\) is a mean-preserving spread of \(\pi\); we omit this direction.)

Proof of Proposition 2.9

\(\E[u(X+Z)]=\E\big[\E[u(X+Z)\mid X]\big]\le\E\big[u(X+\E[Z\mid X])\big] =\E[u(X)]\), by conditional Jensen’s inequality.

2.7.4. Rabin’s calibration theorem#

Theorem 2.3 (Rabin 2000, informal)

Suppose an expected-utility maximiser with increasing concave \(u\) turns down a 50–50 bet to lose £100 or gain £110 at every initial wealth level. Then she also turns down a 50–50 bet to lose £1,000 or gain any amount.

Idea of the proof. Rejecting the small bet at wealth \(w\) says that the utility gained over \([w,w+110]\) is less than that lost over \([w-100,w]\). By concavity, marginal utility at \(w+110\) is at most \(\frac{100}{110}\) of marginal utility at \(w-100\). Since this holds at every \(w\), marginal utility falls by a factor \(\frac{10}{11}\) every £210, geometrically. So the total utility of any gain is bounded, and a modest loss outweighs it. The theorem says that expected utility cannot attribute small-stakes risk aversion to the curvature of \(u\) over wealth without implying absurd large-stakes risk aversion. Example 2.6 is a field version of the same point.

2.7.5. Beyond expected utility#

Rank-dependent utility evaluates a lottery with outcomes \(x_1<\dots<x_n\) by \(\sum_iw_i\,u(x_i)\), where the decision weights \(w_i\) come from a probability weighting function \(q\) applied to the cumulative distribution: \(w_i=q(\pi_i+\dots+\pi_n)-q(\pi_{i+1}+\dots+\pi_n)\). With \(q(p)=p\) this is expected utility. An inverse-S-shaped \(q\), which overweights small probabilities and the step from 99% to 100%, explains the Allais pattern (Exercise Exercise 2.16). Prospect theory adds a reference point and loss aversion. Further reading: Mas-Colell, Whinston and Green (1995, ch. 6); Gollier (2001); Wakker (2010).
