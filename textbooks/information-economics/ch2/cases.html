2.6.1. Case 1: the Allais paradox#

In the early 1950s Maurice Allais put a pair of choices to leading decision theorists, including Leonard Savage, an architect of expected utility. Many of them made choices that violate independence, Savage included (he later said he had made a mistake). Kahneman and Tversky (1979) gave a version to students. The amounts were in Israeli pounds; the probabilities are what matter.

First choice

Second choice

\(A\):

\(2500\) w.p. \(0.33\), \(2400\) w.p. \(0.66\), \(0\) w.p. \(0.01\)

\(C\):

\(2500\) w.p. \(0.33\), \(0\) w.p. \(0.67\)

\(B\):

\(2400\) for sure

\(D\):

\(2400\) w.p. \(0.34\), \(0\) w.p. \(0.66\)

82% chose \(B\)

83% chose \(C\)

Proposition 2.7 (The Allais choices violate expected utility)

Under expected utility with any \(u\), \(B\succ A\) if and only if \(D\succ C\).

Proof of Proposition 2.7

\[\begin{split} \begin{align*} U(B)-U(A)&=u(2400)-0.33u(2500)-0.66u(2400)-0.01u(0)\\ &=0.34u(2400)-0.33u(2500)-0.01u(0),\\ U(D)-U(C)&=0.34u(2400)+0.66u(0)-0.33u(2500)-0.67u(0)\\ &=0.34u(2400)-0.33u(2500)-0.01u(0). \end{align*} \end{split}\]

The two differences are the same number, so they have the same sign.

Why this step?

The proof is pure T1. Both pairs differ only by a common consequence: a \(0.66\) chance of \(2400\) in the first pair, of \(0\) in the second. Independence says a common consequence cannot matter. In the triangle of Fig. 2.1 the segments \(BA\) and \(DC\) are parallel.

What it shows. The popular pattern, \(B\) and \(C\), is inconsistent with any expected-utility maximiser, risk averse or not. The explanation most supported by later work is the certainty effect: moving from a 99% to a 100% chance of winning is valued far more than moving from 33% to 34%. Probabilities are not weighted linearly, as expected utility requires; certainty is special. Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) and rank-dependent utility (Quiggin 1982) build this in with a probability weighting function.

2.6.2. Case 2: deductibles in home insurance#

Proposition 2.6 says that when premiums are loaded people should choose deductibles, and Proposition 2.4(c) says they should be nearly risk neutral over small risks. Sydnor (2010) tested this on about 50,000 homeowners’ insurance policies from one large US insurer. Each customer chose a deductible of $1,000, $500, $250 or $100.

  • Only 17% chose the $1,000 deductible; 48% chose $500 and 35% chose $250.

  • Reducing the deductible from $1,000 to $500 cost about $100 a year in extra premium.

  • The claim rate was about 4% a year. So the lower deductible pays out an extra $500 with probability about \(0.043\): an expected $21.50, for which customers paid about $100.

Example 2.6 (What risk aversion would that take?)

Suppose a customer has CARA utility with coefficient \(\theta\) (in units of \(1/\$\)). She prefers the $500 deductible if paying $100 for sure is better than bearing an extra $500 loss with probability \(0.043\):

\[ e^{100\theta}\ \le\ 0.957+0.043\,e^{500\theta}. \]

This needs \(\theta\ge0.006\). But a person with \(\theta=0.006\) would turn down a 50–50 bet to lose $1,000 or win any amount, even a million: the bet’s expected utility is at most \(\tfrac1\theta\big(1-\tfrac12e^{6}\big)<0=u(0)\). Using wealth-based CRRA utility instead, Sydnor finds implied coefficients of relative risk aversion in the hundreds, against estimates below 10 from almost every other source.

Verdict. Expected utility gets the direction of insurance demand right (U4, U6) and gives the right benchmark for the rest of this book. Its quantitative predictions about small risks and about probabilities fail in a systematic way. Rabin (2000) shows this is unavoidable: any expected-utility maximiser who turns down small favourable gambles at all wealth levels must turn down enormously favourable large ones (Generalisations). The usual readings are probability weighting and loss aversion (people evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point), or that insurance buyers misjudge claim probabilities. For our purposes the lesson is that expected utility describes well how incentives and risk interact in large decisions, which is how Chapters 3–5 use it.
