3.7. Generalisations#

Advanced: for research students

This section relaxes the two-outcome structure, adds extra signals, introduces limited liability, and points to the continuous-effort literature. The proofs use the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions.

Why generalise? The two-outcome, two-action model is chosen because it is the smallest one in which the insurance–incentive trade-off appears. That smallness has a cost. With two outcomes, “pay more after the good outcome” and “pay according to the evidence of effort” are the same instruction, so the model cannot tell us which one is the real logic of the contract. With two outcomes and nothing else observed, the model also cannot say what the principal should do with extra information. This section answers both questions. The answer, in one sentence: the optimal contract rewards outcomes in proportion to how strongly they indicate that the agent took the desired action. Everything else in the two-outcome model, including the binding constraints, the agency cost as a Jensen gap and the under-provision of effort, survives.

3.7.1. Many outcomes: the Grossman–Hart approach#

Let output take \(n\ge2\) values \(y_1<\dots<y_n\), with \(p_i^H\coloneqq\Pr(y_i\mid\ah)>0\) and \(p_i^L\coloneqq\Pr(y_i\mid\al)>0\), and \(p^H\ne p^L\). Define the likelihood ratio \(\ell_i\coloneqq p_i^L/p_i^H\). A contract is \(z=(z_1,\dots,z_n)\) with \(z_i=u(w_i)\). To induce \(\ah\) the principal solves

(3.7)#\[ \min_{z\in\R^n}\sum_i p_i^H h(z_i)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i p_i^H z_i-c\ge\uo,\qquad \sum_i (p_i^H-p_i^L)z_i\ge c . \]

Proof toolkit: why the utility-space formulation is so powerful

In wages, (IC) is \(\sum_i(p_i^H-p_i^L)u(w_i)\ge c\), which is a non-convex constraint. In utilities it is linear. With a convex objective and linear constraints, (3.7) is a convex program: the KKT conditions are necessary (linear constraints need no further constraint qualification) and sufficient (convexity). This observation is due to Grossman and Hart (1983).

Theorem 3.3 (Optimal contract with \(n\) outcomes)

Suppose \(h\) is differentiable and (3.7) has an interior solution \(z^*\). Then there are multipliers \(\lambda>0\) and \(\nu>0\) such that

(3.8)#\[ \frac{1}{u'(w_i^*)}=h'(z_i^*)=\lambda+\nu\,\big(1-\ell_i\big) \qquad\text{for every } i . \]

Both constraints bind, and \(z^*\) is unique.

Proof strategy (Theorem 3.3)

Write the KKT conditions. To show the multipliers are strictly positive we exploit two identities: \(\sum_i p_i^H=1\) and \(\sum_i p_i^H(1-\ell_i)=\sum_i(p_i^H-p_i^L)=0\). Taking a probability-weighted sum of the first-order conditions makes the \(\nu\) term vanish, which isolates \(\lambda\).

Proof of Theorem 3.3

The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L=\sum_i p_i^H h(z_i)-\lambda\big(\sum_ip_i^Hz_i-c-\uo\big) -\nu\big(\sum_i(p_i^H-p_i^L)z_i-c\big)\) with \(\lambda,\nu\ge0\). The first-order conditions are \(p_i^H h'(z_i)=\lambda p_i^H+\nu(p_i^H-p_i^L)\). Dividing by \(p_i^H>0\) gives (3.8).

\(\lambda>0\). Multiply (3.8) by \(p_i^H\) and sum: \(\sum_ip_i^Hh'(z_i)=\lambda+\nu\sum_i(p_i^H-p_i^L)=\lambda\). The left side is strictly positive (Lemma 3.1), so \(\lambda>0\), and by complementary slackness (PC) binds.

\(\nu>0\). If \(\nu=0\) then \(h'(z_i)=\lambda\) for all \(i\); since \(h'\) is strictly increasing, all \(z_i\) are equal. But then \(\sum_i(p_i^H-p_i^L)z_i=0<c\) and (IC) fails. So \(\nu>0\), and (IC) binds.

Uniqueness. The objective is strictly convex and the feasible set is convex.

Equation (3.8) is the Holmström–Mirrlees condition. Its message: pay is high after outcomes that are unlikely under the low action. The principal is not rewarding output as such; she is using output as statistical evidence about effort.

Definition 3.4 (MLRP)

The distributions satisfy the monotone likelihood ratio property if \(\ell_i=p_i^L/p_i^H\) is decreasing in \(i\): higher output is relatively more likely under high effort.

Corollary 3.2 (Monotone pay)

Under MLRP the optimal wage \(w_i^*\) is increasing in output. Without MLRP the optimal wage need not be monotone.

Proof of Corollary 3.2

By (3.8) with \(\nu>0\), \(h'(z_i^*)\) is a decreasing function of \(\ell_i\). Under MLRP \(\ell_i\) falls with \(i\), so \(h'(z_i^*)\) rises. Since \(h'\) is strictly increasing (strict convexity), \(z_i^*\) rises, and so does \(w_i^*=h(z_i^*)\). Exercise 3.14 gives a counterexample without MLRP.

Proposition 3.3 (Agency cost with square-root utility)

Let \(u(w)=\sqrt w\) and assume interiority. Then

\[ AC=\frac{c^2}{\chi^2},\qquad \chi^2\coloneqq\sum_i\frac{(p_i^H-p_i^L)^2}{p_i^H}=\E_H\big[(1-\ell)^2\big]. \]

Proof of Proposition 3.3

With \(h(z)=z^2\), (3.8) reads \(2z_i=\lambda+\nu(1-\ell_i)\). Taking \(\E_H\) and using \(\E_H[1-\ell]=0\) gives \(\lambda/2=\E_H[z]=\uo+c\). So \(z_i=(\uo+c)+k(1-\ell_i)\) with \(k=\nu/2\). Binding (IC) is \(\sum_ip_i^H(1-\ell_i)z_i=c\), i.e. \(k\,\E_H[(1-\ell)^2]=c\), so \(k=c/\chi^2\). Finally \(AC=\E_H[z^2]-(\E_Hz)^2=\Var_H(z)=k^2\chi^2=c^2/\chi^2\).

With two outcomes \(\chi^2=\Dpi^2/[\muh(1-\muh)]\), which recovers (3.6). The \(\chi^2\)-divergence measures how well the observable statistic discriminates between the actions. The better the evidence, the cheaper the incentives.

3.7.2. The informativeness principle#

Suppose that besides output the principal can observe a signal \(s\) (a telematics score, a supervisor’s report). Let outcomes be pairs \(j=(y,s)\), finitely many, with \(p^a_j>0\). Everything in Many outcomes: the Grossman–Hart approach applies with \(j\) in place of \(i\). Say that \(s\) is uninformative given \(y\) if \(p^a(y,s)=p^a(y)\,q(s\mid y)\) with \(q\) not depending on \(a\).

Theorem 3.4 (Informativeness principle; Holmström 1979)

In the optimal contract inducing \(\ah\), pay depends on \(s\) if and only if \(s\) is informative given \(y\): there are \(y,s,s'\) with \(\ell(y,s)\ne\ell(y,s')\). If \(s\) is informative, using it strictly lowers the principal’s cost.

Proof of Theorem 3.4

By (3.8), \(z^*_{(y,s)}\) is a strictly decreasing function of \(\ell(y,s)\) (because \(\nu>0\) and \(h'\) is strictly increasing). If \(s\) is uninformative, \(\ell(y,s)=p^L(y)q(s\mid y)/[p^H(y)q(s\mid y)]=\ell(y)\) for all \(s\), so \(z^*\) does not depend on \(s\). If \(\ell(y,s)\ne\ell(y,s')\) then \(z^*_{(y,s)}\ne z^*_{(y,s')}\), so the optimal contract depends on \(s\). In that case the best contract that ignores \(s\) is feasible in the larger problem but violates its first-order conditions; since the optimum is unique, the contract that ignores \(s\) is strictly costlier.

This is the theory behind Progressive: the telematics score is informative about care beyond whether an accident happened, so the optimal policy conditions on it.

3.7.3. Limited liability and rents#

Suppose pay cannot fall below a floor: \(z_b\ge\underline z\) (a minimum wage, a guarantee, bankruptcy). In the two-outcome model:

Proposition 3.4 (Rents from a floor)

If \(\underline z>\uo-\mul c/\Dpi\), the cheapest contract inducing \(\ah\) is \(z_b=\underline z\), \(z_g=\underline z+c/\Dpi\). (IC) binds, (PC) is slack, and the agent earns a rent

\[ R=\underline z+\mul\frac{c}{\Dpi}-\uo>0 . \]

Proof of Proposition 3.4

For any feasible contract, \(z_g\ge z_b+c/\Dpi\ge\underline z+c/\Dpi\) and \(z_b\ge\underline z\). Since \(h\) is increasing, the expected wage bill is at least that of \((\underline z+c/\Dpi,\ \underline z)\). This contract satisfies the floor and (IC), and its expected utility net of \(c\) is \(\underline z+\muh c/\Dpi-c=\underline z+\mul c/\Dpi>\uo\) (using \(\muh-\Dpi=\mul\)), so (PC) holds. It is therefore optimal, and \(R\) is the slack in (PC).

Under the NHS scheme the core payment set \(\underline z=\uo\), so the rent is \(\mul c/\Dpi=0.35\), exactly as in Example 3.2. This is one reason P4 fails. The same logic with a risk-neutral agent and \(w\ge0\) is the standard model of efficiency wages and of why workers with no wealth earn rents (Innes 1990; Laffont and Martimort 2002, ch. 4).

3.7.4. Continuous effort and the first-order approach#

With effort \(a\in[0,\bar a]\) and density \(f(y\mid a)\), the natural move is to replace the incentive constraint (“\(a\) is optimal for the agent”) with its first-order condition. This first-order approach gives the continuous analogue of (3.8),

\[ \frac{1}{u'(w(y))}=\lambda+\nu\,\frac{f_a(y\mid a)}{f(y\mid a)}, \]

where \(f_a/f\) is the score (the derivative of the log-likelihood). But the first-order condition is necessary, not sufficient: the agent’s problem need not be concave, and the approach can fail (Mirrlees 1975). Rogerson (1985) shows it is valid under MLRP together with the convexity of the distribution function condition. Jewitt (1988) gives weaker conditions. In continuous time with Brownian noise the optimal contract can be linear in output (Holmström and Milgrom 1987), and with several tasks, incentives on a measurable task can crowd out effort on an unmeasurable one (Holmström and Milgrom 1991). The fall in unpaid aspects of GP care after 2004 (The model meets the data: two cases) is an instance of that last point.

Further reading. Bolton and Dewatripont, Contract Theory (2005), ch. 4; Laffont and Martimort, The Theory of Incentives (2002), chs. 4–5; Salanié, The Economics of Contracts (2005), ch. 5.
