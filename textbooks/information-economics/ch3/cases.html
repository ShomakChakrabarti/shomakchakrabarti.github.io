3.6. The model meets the data: two cases#

A model is a claim about how the world works. To find out whether it is a good claim we need to turn it into statements that data could contradict. This section lists seven such predictions and confronts them with evidence from two well-documented cases. In one, the principal changes the contract; in the other, she changes the information. Together they cover both remedies the model suggests.

How to read the verdicts. We mark a prediction Holds when it has been tested directly in data and survived; Fits when the evidence is consistent with it but is not a direct test; In part when some of it is borne out and some is not; and Fails when the evidence contradicts it. A failed prediction is not a reason to throw the model away. It is a pointer to the assumption that does not hold in that setting, which is often the most useful thing the exercise produces. Throughout, separate the mechanism (how incentives work) from the conclusion (who gets the surplus): the two can fare very differently.

3.6.1. Case 1: NHS family doctors, paying for quality#

Until 2004 an NHS family practice in England was paid mainly by capitation: a fixed sum for each patient on its list, adjusted for age and need. A practice that called its diabetic patients in every few months, checked their blood sugar and blood pressure and adjusted their drugs was paid the same as one that saw them only when they turned up ill. The NHS could see some results in patients’ records, but it could not tell good luck from good care.

In April 2004 a new contract added the Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF). A practice could earn up to 1,050 points on 146 indicators. Most were clinical: the share of patients with diabetes whose blood sugar was below a target, the share of patients with high blood pressure whose last reading was below 150/90, the share of heart-attack survivors on the recommended drugs. For each indicator, payment rose in proportion to the share of eligible patients reaching it, from a lower threshold to an upper one (for example from 25% to 90%). A point was worth £77.50 to an average practice in 2004/05 and about £125 from 2005/06, and the scheme cost about £1 billion a year. Practices could exclude (“exception report”) patients for whom an indicator was unsuitable, for example those who refused treatment. The core capitation payment did not fall: QOF was paid on top.

What happened. Practices in England earned an average of 958.7 of the 1,050 points in the first year (91%), against the 75% the government had budgeted for. The new contract as a whole cost £1.76 billion more than planned, and the average income of a GP partner rose by 58% in three years, from £72,000 in 2002/03 to £114,000 in 2005/06 (National Audit Office 2008). (The contract also let practices hand over out-of-hours care, so not all of this rise was paid for quality.) Measured care improved: Campbell et al. (2009) find that the rate of improvement in diabetes and asthma care sped up when the scheme began, then levelled off. But several things happened that the simple model does not predict.

  • Paying for care already given. Much of the money paid for quality that practices were already delivering, which is why achievement was so far above the budget.

  • What is not measured suffers. Some aspects of care that carried no points got worse, and continuity of care (seeing the same doctor) fell (Campbell et al. 2009).

  • Measures can be managed. Payment depended on a ratio: patients reaching a target over patients eligible. Practices could raise the ratio by excluding patients or by how they recorded disease. Most exclusions were for good clinical reasons (about 5% of patients; Doran et al. 2008), but Gravelle, Sutton and Ma (2010) find patterns in the data consistent with some gaming.

  • Health outcomes. Ryan et al. (2016) find no detectable effect of the scheme on population mortality.

Scotland abolished QOF in 2016. England kept it but retired 32 indicators in 2025/26, moving part of the money back into the core payment.

Scorecard. P2 (flat pay gives the low action) is consistent with the speed-up in measured care once it was paid for, though practices’ achievement before 2004 was already high, so capitation did not produce the pure low action of the model. P3 and P5 hold only in part: QOF was all carrot and no stick. P4 fails clearly: GPs gained. P7 is in doubt, because the scheme paid heavily for care that was already being given and had no detectable effect on mortality. Two features lie outside the model. Multitasking: the GP’s job has many tasks, and paying for the measured ones draws effort away from the rest (Holmström and Milgrom 1991). Manipulable measures: the model assumes output is verifiable, but here the agent keeps the records the payment is based on.

Hospitals too. In 2008 the 24 NHS hospitals in the North West of England joined Advancing Quality, which paid bonuses to the hospitals that scored best on measured care for pneumonia, heart failure and heart attack. Sutton et al. (2012) estimate about 890 fewer deaths in the first 18 months, compared with hospitals elsewhere in England. Kristensen et al. (2014) find that the relative gain had disappeared after three and a half years, partly because care elsewhere caught up. Midway through, the bonuses were replaced with penalties for missing targets.

3.6.2. Case 2: Progressive Insurance, changing the information#

An insurer cannot see how you drive; it can see whether you crash. So the standard car-insurance policy punishes the bad outcome: a deductible on damage to your own car, and a surcharge at renewal after a claim. That is Theorem 3.2(a): partial insurance with a stick in the bad state.

Progressive’s idea was to put a device in the car and price the action. A usage-based policy was piloted in Texas by 2002; in 2004 TripSense recorded mileage, time of day, speed and braking for 5,000 customers in Minnesota; in 2011 Snapshot launched nationally, setting a discount of up to 30% from six months of recorded driving.

Jin and Vasserman (2021) study a major US insurer with over a million customers. Monitoring is offered once, to new customers, for about five months. The same drivers are about 30% safer while monitored than afterwards. Only 15–21% opt in, and those who do were safer to begin with. The insurer pays for consent with discounts, and drivers behave as if being monitored costs them about $113 per period. Total surplus rises by about $9.79 per person per year.

Without it, \(c/\Dpi=2\), so \(z_g^*=9.2+0.3\cdot2=9.8\) (wealth \(96.04\), premium \(3.96\)) and \(z_b^*=9.2-0.7\cdot2=7.8\) (wealth \(60.84=36+\text{payout}-3.96\), payout \(28.8\)). Without the device the driver keeps 55% of the loss (P5). The device is worth \(1.44-1.08=0.36=0.9\times0.1\times2^2\) to the insurer (P6), exactly (3.6).

Why so few drivers opt in. In the model the insurer keeps the whole \(0.36\) and every driver accepts, since (PC) binds either way. Suppose instead that a driver needs compensation \(m\) (in money) to put up with the device. Monitoring is worth offering if and only if \(0.36-m\ge0\): for \(m<0.36\) the device is used and the driver is paid \(m\); for \(m>0.36\) the deductible is cheaper. In the data the average driver requires about $113 per period, so only the drivers with the most to gain opt in. They can also take their business to a competitor, so they keep part of the gain: P4 fails here too.

Holds: tested in the data. Fits: consistent with the data but not a direct test. In part: true of part of the prediction only.

  1. Incentives move what they measure. Paying for measured care raised measured care, and watching drivers made them drive more carefully (P2, P3).

  2. The surplus-division result does not hold. P4 fails both times. Real principals do not make take-it-or-leave-it offers: the GP contract was negotiated with the doctors’ union, and drivers can go to another insurer. Floors on pay create rents (Limited liability and rents).

  3. What is measured is not all that matters. When the agent has several tasks, or can influence the measure itself, paying for the measure can draw effort away from what the principal really wants (Generalisations).

  4. Both cases run into hidden information. Which patients a practice records and excludes, and which drivers opt into monitoring, are about what the agent knows. That is adverse selection and signalling, the subjects of Chapters 4 and 5.
