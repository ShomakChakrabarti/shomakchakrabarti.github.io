3.1. Introduction#

3.1.1. A family doctor, a driver and a tenant#

In 2003 the NHS paid a family doctor (GP) in England mainly according to how many patients were registered with his practice. Whether his patients with diabetes had their blood sugar checked and controlled, whether those with high blood pressure had it brought down, whether a patient who had survived a heart attack was on the right drugs: none of this changed his income. The NHS could see some of the results, such as how many of the practice’s patients had their condition under control. What it could not see was why the results were what they were. Poor results might mean the practice rarely called patients in for reviews or adjusted their treatment. They might equally mean an older, poorer or sicker list of patients, patients who did not take their medicines, or a hard winter. In 2004 the NHS rewrote the contract to pay for results; The model meets the data: two cases shows what happened.

A car owner who buys comprehensive insurance faces the same structure from the other side. The insurer sees whether she crashes. It does not see how fast she drove, how often she checked her mirrors or whether she drove home tired. A crash might be the result of careless driving or of plain bad luck.

A landowner who lets a field to a tenant farmer sees the harvest at the end of the season but not the hours that went into weeding, irrigation and pest control. A poor harvest might be a lazy season or a dry one.

These three situations share a structure that this chapter studies.

  • One party, the principal, needs another, the agent, to take an action on her behalf: work fast, drive carefully, tend the field.

  • The action is costly to the agent, so left to himself he would rather take less of it.

  • The principal cannot observe the action, or cannot prove in court what it was. So the contract cannot simply say “work hard”.

  • The principal can observe an outcome that the action influences: patients’ test results, an accident claim, the harvest. But the outcome is also affected by chance, so it is only a noisy signal of the action.

3.1.2. What “moral hazard” means#

The name comes from the insurance industry, where it described the worry that being insured makes people less careful. Economists took up the term in the 1960s, when Arrow (1963) and Pauly (1968) pointed out that this is not a matter of morals at all: it is the ordinary response of a rational person to a contract that shields him from the consequences of his actions. Holmström (1979) and Grossman and Hart (1983) turned the idea into the principal–agent model that we study here.

Definition 3.1 (Moral hazard)

Moral hazard is a situation in which, after a contract has been signed, one party takes an action that affects the other party’s payoff and that the other party cannot observe or verify. It is also called the problem of hidden action.

Moral hazard is everywhere economic activity is delegated. The table below lists some of the most studied cases. In each row, ask yourself what the outcome is, and why it is only a noisy measure of the action.

In practice the two problems often appear together. The case studies in The model meets the data: two cases show this. Under the NHS scheme, part of the measured improvement came from which patients practices recorded and excluded, something only the practice knew (hidden information), rather than from better care (hidden action). And the drivers who accept a telematics box are the ones who were safer to begin with.

3.1.4. The central trade-off: insurance versus incentives#

Chapter 2 showed that a risk-averse person who can buy actuarially fair insurance buys full insurance. Moral hazard is the reason the real world rarely looks like that. The tension is easy to see in the GP’s case.

  • A flat payment per registered patient (capitation) gives the GP perfect insurance: his income does not depend on how sick his patients happen to be or on a bad flu season. But it does not depend on the care he gives either, so he has no financial reason to do the time-consuming work of calling patients in and adjusting their treatment.

  • Pay that depends entirely on patients’ results gives him strong incentives. But it also makes his income depend on his patients’ age and circumstances, on whether they take their medicines and on epidemics, none of which he controls and all of which he dislikes because he is risk-averse. He must be compensated for that risk.

The principal cannot have both perfect insurance and perfect incentives. The optimal contract is a compromise: pay depends on output just enough to make effort worthwhile, and no more. Measured against a world where effort could be seen, this compromise costs something. The main results of the chapter characterise the compromise and measure that cost.

3.1.5. What you will learn#

After working through this chapter you should be able to:

  1. set up a principal–agent problem, stating the participation and incentive constraints and explaining what each one means;

  2. solve for the optimal contract when the action is contractible and when it is hidden, and prove that your solution is optimal;

  3. explain why hidden action forces the agent to bear risk, and compute the cost of that risk;

  4. explain why hidden action can lead to too little effort but never too much;

  5. map the model onto real contracts (piece rates, bonuses, deductibles, telematics) and judge which predictions hold in data;

  6. (Advanced) derive the optimal contract with many outcomes and explain the role of likelihood ratios and of extra signals.

What you need. Expected utility, risk aversion and certainty equivalents from Chapter 2, and Jensen’s inequality. The advanced section uses Lagrangians and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions.

Roadmap. The model sets out the model and explains each assumption. The first best: contractible actions solves the benchmark in which the action is contractible. The second best: hidden actions solves the problem when the action is hidden. Welfare and the cost of hidden action measures what is lost. The model meets the data: two cases takes the predictions to the NHS’s pay-for-performance scheme for GPs and to Progressive Insurance. Generalisations generalises the model for research students. Exercises and corrections to the lecture slides close the chapter.

Proof toolkit: three techniques used throughout this chapter

Almost every proof in this chapter is built from three moves. Learn to spot them.

  • (T1) Perturbation (“if not, improve”). To show a constraint binds at an optimum, suppose it is slack, construct a small change that stays feasible and strictly improves the objective, and conclude the original point was not optimal.

  • (T2) Jensen’s inequality. For a strictly convex function \(h\) and a non-degenerate random variable \(Z\), \(\E[h(Z)] > h(\E[Z])\). Here \(h\) converts utility into money, so Jensen says that a risky promise of utility costs more money than a safe one with the same mean.

  • (T3) Relaxation. To solve a hard problem, drop a constraint, solve the easier problem, then check whether the solution happens to satisfy the constraint you dropped. If it does, it solves the original problem too.
