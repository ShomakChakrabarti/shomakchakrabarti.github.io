The first best: contractible actions

3.3. The first best: contractible actions#

It seems intuitively clear that hidden actions impose some sort of “inefficiency” in our setting. Our objective is to quantify this inefficiency and understand who bears its cost. To properly analyse this, we first need to answer the question - inefficient relative to what? A natural starting point

We start with a world that does not exist: one in which the principal can write the action into the contract. There are two reasons to study it. First, it is the benchmark. The cost of hidden action can only be measured against what the principal could achieve if the action were not hidden, so we need to know what that is. Second, it isolates one of the two jobs a contract does. With the action contractible, incentives are taken care of by the courts, and the contract only has to share risk. Seeing how it does that, on its own, makes it much easier to see what goes wrong when it has to do both jobs at once.

Formally, suppose the action is contractible: a court can verify which action was taken, so a contract can say “take action \(a\); if you do not, you are paid nothing”. Observable here does not mean literally watched at every moment. It means the action can be legally enforced. The term first best refers to the best outcome achievable with full information; anything achievable only with less information is a second best.

What should we expect? Chapter 2 showed that a risk-averse person offered actuarially fair insurance chooses to be fully insured. Here the risk-neutral principal plays the role of the insurer. If nothing else gets in the way, we should expect her to take all the output risk and pay the agent a fixed wage. The theorem confirms this.

A first-best contract is a triple \((a,z_g,z_b)\). Since a deviation from the stipulated action can be punished, only participation matters:

\[ \mu(a)z_g+(1-\mu(a))z_b-c(a)\;\ge\;\uo . \tag{PC$_a$} \]

Theorem 3.1 (Full insurance under contractible actions)

Suppose the action is contractible. Then:

  • (a) For each action \(a\) the optimal contract gives the agent full insurance, \(z_g=z_b=\uo+c(a)\). So the agent is paid a flat wage \(h(\uo+c)\) for the high action and \(h(\uo)\) for the low action.

  • (b) The participation constraint binds: the agent gets exactly \(\uo\).

  • (c) The principal stipulates the high action if and only if

    (3.1)#\[ \Dpi\,\Dy \;\ge\; h(\uo+c)-h(\uo). \]

Proof strategy (Theorem 3.1)

Fix the action, so the problem is to minimise the expected wage bill subject to (PC\(_a\)). Step 1 shows the constraint binds by perturbation (T1): lower both utilities by the same amount. Step 2 uses Jensen (T2): given the mean utility, the cheapest way to deliver it is without risk. Step 3 compares the two optimal costs to choose the action.

Proof of Theorem 3.1

Fix \(a\) and write \(\mu=\mu(a)\). The principal solves

\[ \min_{z_g,z_b}\; \mu\,h(z_g)+(1-\mu)\,h(z_b) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mu z_g+(1-\mu)z_b-c(a)\ge \uo . \]

Step 1: (PC\(_a\)) binds. Suppose \((z_g,z_b)\) is optimal but \(s\coloneqq\mu z_g+(1-\mu)z_b-c(a)-\uo>0\). Let \(\tilde z_i=z_i-s\) for both \(i\).

Why this step?

We lower utility in both states by the same amount, so mean utility falls by exactly \(s\) and the constraint now holds with equality. Nothing else about the contract changes.

Then \(\mu\tilde z_g+(1-\mu)\tilde z_b-c(a)=\uo\), so the new contract is feasible, and because \(h\) is strictly increasing, \(h(\tilde z_i)<h(z_i)\) for both \(i\), so the expected wage bill is strictly lower. This contradicts optimality.

Step 2: full insurance. By Step 1 every optimal contract has \(\E[z]=\mu z_g+(1-\mu)z_b=\uo+c(a)\). For any such contract, Jensen’s inequality for the convex \(h\) gives

(3.2)#\[ \mu\,h(z_g)+(1-\mu)\,h(z_b)\;\ge\;h\big(\mu z_g+(1-\mu)z_b\big) = h\big(\uo+c(a)\big), \]

Why this step?

This gives a lower bound on the cost of every feasible contract. If we can find a contract that attains the bound, it must be optimal.

The full-insurance contract \(z_g=z_b=\uo+c(a)\) attains the bound, so it is optimal. Because \(h\) is strictly convex and \(\mu\in(0,1)\) (Assumption 3.1), (3.2) is strict whenever \(z_g\ne z_b\), so full insurance is the unique optimum. This proves (a) and (b).

Step 3: choice of action. The principal’s profit from the optimal high-action contract is \(\muh y_g+(1-\muh)y_b-h(\uo+c)\), and from the low-action contract \(\mul y_g+(1-\mul)y_b-h(\uo)\). The first is at least the second if and only if

\[ (\muh-\mul)(y_g-y_b)\;\ge\;h(\uo+c)-h(\uo), \]

which is (3.1).

Common mistake

In Step 3 the low-action profit uses the low-action probability \(\mul\). Using \(\muh\) for both actions (as one slide does) makes the output terms cancel and the comparison meaningless. The left side of (3.1) is the gain in expected output from the high action; the right side is the extra wage needed to compensate for its cost.

Why full insurance and links to Chapter [ch:cu]? The principal is risk-neutral and the agent is risk-averse. Any risk the agent bears must be paid for: he values a lottery over utilities at its certainty equivalent, and by Jensen the expected wage of a risky contract exceeds the wage that delivers the same expected utility for sure. When actions are contractible there is no reason to impose risk, so efficient risk sharing puts all of it on the risk-neutral party.

Who gets the surplus? Because the principal makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer and the agent has no private information, the agent is held to his outside option and the principal keeps the entire surplus. In the language of pricing, this is first-degree (perfect) price discrimination.

Total surplus. Since the agent always gets \(\uo\), ex-ante social welfare under action \(a\) is

\[ S^{\FB}(a)=\uo+\mu(a)y_g+(1-\mu(a))y_b-h\big(\uo+c(a)\big), \]

so the action that maximises welfare is exactly the one the principal chooses. The first best is efficient.

Reading the theorem part by part.

  • Part (a) says pay does not depend on output. Output still varies, but the principal absorbs all of the variation. The agent is paid for doing what the contract says, not for what happens.

  • Part (b) says the agent is paid exactly enough to accept. For the high action, \(h(\uo+c)\) is his outside wage \(h(\uo)\) plus a compensating differential for effort: the money that makes him just willing to bear the cost \(c\).

  • Part (c) is a cost–benefit test. The left side \(\Dpi\Dy\) is the extra expected output the high action produces; the right side is the extra wage needed to compensate the agent for it. The principal buys effort exactly when it is worth more than it costs.

In the running example. The first-best high-action contract pays a flat \(h(10.3)=106.09\) a year; the low-action contract pays 100. Since \(\Dpi\Dy=60\ge6.09\), the NHS stipulates proactive care and gets \(330-106.09=223.91\) a year in health net of pay, against \(270-100=170\) with the low action.
