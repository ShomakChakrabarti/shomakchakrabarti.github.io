3.2. The model#

3.2.1. The model in one paragraph#

A risk-neutral principal hires a risk-averse agent. The agent chooses one of two actions, high or low; the high action costs him more but makes a good outcome more likely. Output is either good or bad, and the principal sees it; she does not see the action. The principal offers a wage schedule that can depend on output, on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. The agent accepts or rejects; if he accepts he chooses his action, output is realised and he is paid.

The rest of this section explains each piece of that paragraph and why it is there. A good habit when you meet any model is to ask, for each assumption, what would change if it were dropped? The answers are collected in The three ingredients of moral hazard.

3.2.2. Players#

There are two players: a Principal (she) and an Agent (he).[1] The principal owns a productive venture. This could be a firm, an insurance pool, or a plot of farmland. The output of this venture is observable to every party in this environment. The agent’s action affects how much output the venture produces.

3.2.3. Outcomes, actions and noise#

The venture’s output takes one of two values, \(y\in\{y_b,y_g\}\), with \(y_g>y_b\). We interpret \(y_g\) as “good” output and \(y_b\) as “bad” output. We write

\[ \Dy \coloneqq y_g-y_b>0 \]

for the gain from a good output. The agent chooses an action \(a\in\{\al,\ah\}\) with \(\ah>\al\). We interpret \(\ah\) as the “high” action and \(\al\) as the “low” action Importantly, we assume that the agent’s action does not determine output directly. Actions rather shifts the probability of the good output:

\[ \underbrace{\Pr(y_g\mid \ah)=\muh}_{\text{prob. of good output from high action}},\qquad \underbrace{\Pr(y_g\mid\al)=\mul}_{\text{prob. of good output from low action}}, \qquad \Dpi\coloneqq \muh-\mul>0 . \]

Write \(\mu(a)\) for \(\Pr(y_g\mid a)\). We can interpret the agent’s action as the effort or care that the agent puts into the production process. If he puts in more care, more likely is the output going to be good!

Interpreting the action. “Action” is deliberately broad. It is anything the agent does that is costly to him, benefits the principal, and cannot be written into a contract: the effort a doctor puts into following up patients, the care taken while driving, the attention a manager gives to a project. Two actions are the smallest number that can create a trade-off. Generalisations discusses what changes with many actions and many outcomes; the main lessons survive.

Interpreting the probabilities. The pair \((\muh,\mul)\) describes two things at once. It is a production technology: the high action raises expected output by

\[ \big[\muh y_g+(1-\muh)y_b\big]-\big[\mul y_g+(1-\mul)y_b\big]=\Dpi\,\Dy . \]

It is also a monitoring technology: output is the only evidence the principal has about the action, and \(\Dpi\) measures how good that evidence is. When \(\Dpi\) is close to one, a good outcome almost proves that the agent worked. When \(\Dpi\) is close to zero, output tells the principal almost nothing, because luck swamps effort. We will see that the cost of hidden action is driven by this second role.

Why output must be noisy. The randomness in output is essential. If output were a deterministic function of the action, the principal could infer the action from output perfectly, and a contract on output would be as good as a contract on the action. The noise is what makes the action genuinely hidden.

Assumption 3.1 (Interior probabilities)

\(0<\mul<\muh<1\).

Both outcomes can happen under both actions. Suppose instead that \(\muh=1\): the high action guarantees a good outcome. Then a bad outcome would prove the agent shirked, and the principal could attach a severe penalty to it. A diligent agent would never pay the penalty, so the threat would cost the principal nothing, and the first best could be achieved. Assumption Assumption 3.1 rules out such “smoking gun” outcomes. (Mirrlees (1975) showed that a version of this argument can work even when no outcome is a perfect smoking gun, if punishments can be unboundedly severe; this is one reason the limited-liability case in Limited liability and rents matters.)

3.2.4. Preferences#

Assumption 3.2 (Preferences)

  • (a) The principal is risk-neutral. If she pays wage \(w\) and output is \(y\) her payoff is \(y-w\). Her outside option is \(0\).

  • (b) The agent is risk-averse. If he is paid \(w\) and takes action \(a\) his payoff is \(u(w)-c(a)\), where \(u\) is strictly increasing and strictly concave. His outside option is \(\uo\).

  • (c) The cost of the low action is normalised to zero and the high action costs more: \(c(\al)=0<c(\ah)\eqqcolon c\).

Why is the principal risk-neutral? The principal is usually an owner who holds a diversified portfolio (shareholders), or an institution that pools many independent risks (an insurer). In either case the risk attached to any one agent’s output is small relative to her wealth, and she cares only about its expected value. Chapter 2 explains why a large pool of independent risks makes an insurer behave as if risk-neutral.

Why is the agent risk-averse? The agent’s income from this one relationship is a large part of his wealth, and he cannot easily diversify it: a worker cannot sell shares in his own labour income. Strict concavity of \(u\) means a fixed amount of money is worth less to him the more he already has, so he dislikes variable income.

Why is utility separable? The agent’s payoff \(u(w)-c(a)\) is additively separable in money and effort: the pain of effort does not depend on how much he is paid, and the pleasure of money does not depend on how hard he worked. This is a simplifying assumption. Without it, a richer agent might find effort more or less painful, and the analysis would have to track these “income effects”. With it, the incentive constraint becomes linear in utilities, which is what makes the problem easy to solve.

What does the cost mean? The cost \(c\) is measured in units of utility, not money. It is the agent’s disutility from working at the high action rather than the low one. Only the difference \(c(\ah)-c(\al)\) ever enters the analysis: adding a constant to both costs would shift the agent’s payoff from every contract and action by the same amount, which changes nothing he chooses. That is why we can normalise \(c(\al)=0\) without loss of generality.

What does the outside option mean? The outside option \(\uo\) is the utility the agent gets if he turns the principal down: a job elsewhere, or no insurance. It is often called his reservation utility. It summarises the state of the market for agents. If many practices compete to recruit scarce GPs, \(\uo\) is high; if GPs are plentiful, it is low. In this model the principal can push the agent all the way down to \(\uo\), and no further.

3.2.5. Contracts and what they can depend on#

A contract is offered before output is realised but paid after it, so it can depend on anything that is publicly observable and verifiable when payment is due. Verifiable means that a court, or any third party that enforces the contract, could confirm it.

Observable is not enough. The distinction between observable and verifiable matters in practice. A supervisor may see that a practice’s reviews of its patients are rushed, but if she cannot prove it to a court or an arbitrator, a contract that pays according to her opinion cannot be enforced: the payer would always claim the work was poor, to save money. For our purposes, an action that the principal can see but not prove is just as hidden as one she cannot see at all. When we say an action is “observable” in The first best: contractible actions, we mean that it is contractible.

Two jobs for one contract. When the action is hidden, a contract is a pair of wages \(w=(w_g,w_b)\), one for each output. Because output is random, the wage is a random variable whose distribution the agent controls through his action. The contract therefore does two jobs at once. It shares risk: the more the wage varies with output, the more of the output risk the agent carries. And it provides incentives: the more the wage varies with output, the more the agent gains from making the good outcome likely. Both jobs are done by the same number, the gap between \(w_g\) and \(w_b\). That is why they conflict.

3.2.6. Thinking in utility, not money#

It is much easier to work with the utility the contract promises in each state than with the wage itself. Let \(z_i\coloneqq u(w_i)\) for \(i\in\{g,b\}\) and let

\[ \hinv\coloneqq u^{-1},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad w_i=\hinv(z_i). \]

A contract is then equivalently a pair \((z_g,z_b)\) of promised utilities.

Interpretation of \(h\). Read \(h(z)\) as the money it costs the principal to give the agent utility \(z\). Its shape carries the economics. Because the agent has diminishing marginal utility of money, each extra unit of utility costs more money than the last. For example, with \(u(w)=\sqrt w\) we have \(h(z)=z^2\): raising the agent’s utility from 9 to 10 costs \(100-81=19\), while raising it from 10 to 11 costs \(121-100=21\). So \(h\) is convex. Lemma Lemma 3.1 proves this in general.

Lemma 3.1 (Properties of \(h\))

Under Assumption 3.2, \(h=u^{-1}\) is strictly increasing and strictly convex. If \(u\) is differentiable, \(h'(z)=1/u'(h(z))>0\).

Proof strategy

“Strictly increasing” is inherited directly. For convexity we turn the definition of concavity of \(u\) into a statement about \(h\) by applying the increasing function \(h\) to both sides of an inequality.

Proof of Lemma 3.1

If \(z_1<z_2\) then \(h(z_1)<h(z_2)\), otherwise applying the increasing \(u\) would give \(z_1\ge z_2\). So \(h\) is strictly increasing.

Take \(z_1\neq z_2\) and \(t\in(0,1)\); let \(w_i=h(z_i)\), so \(w_1\ne w_2\). Strict concavity of \(u\) gives

\[ u\big(tw_1+(1-t)w_2\big) > t\,u(w_1)+(1-t)\,u(w_2) = tz_1+(1-t)z_2 . \]

Why this step?

We want an inequality about \(h\), so we apply \(h\) to both sides; it is increasing, so the inequality survives.

Applying \(h\) to both sides, \(t\,h(z_1)+(1-t)\,h(z_2) > h\big(tz_1+(1-t)z_2\big)\), which is strict convexity. The formula for \(h'\) is the inverse function rule.

Why the change of variables helps. In money, the agent’s expected utility \(\mu u(w_g)+(1-\mu)u(w_b)\) is a non-linear function of the contract. In utilities, it is \(\mu z_g+(1-\mu)z_b\), which is linear. So every constraint involving the agent’s preferences becomes a linear constraint, and all of the non-linearity moves into the principal’s cost \(h\). Linear constraints and a convex objective make a problem that is easy to solve and easy to reason about.

The principal’s objective. The principal’s payoff from contract \((z_g,z_b)\) when the agent takes action \(a\) is

\[ \underbrace{\mu(a)y_g+(1-\mu(a))y_b}_{\text{expected output}} \;-\;\underbrace{\big[\mu(a)\,h(z_g)+(1-\mu(a))\,h(z_b)\big]}_{\text{expected wage bill}} . \]

For a fixed action, expected output is a constant, so maximising profit is the same as minimising the expected wage bill. Every proof below starts from this observation.

Assumption 3.3 (Interiority)

Every utility level the principal needs to promise below lies in the range of \(u\). For \(u(w)=\sqrt w\) this means \(z_b\ge 0\), i.e. \(\uo\ge \mul c/\Dpi\).

This is a technical assumption with an economic meaning: the principal is able to pay the agent little enough after a bad outcome. Without it, the wage would have to fall below the lowest wage \(u\) is defined for (for \(\sqrt w\), below zero). Then a limited-liability constraint binds, which changes the results in an interesting way (Limited liability and rents).

3.2.7. Timing#

Reading the timeline. Three features of the timing do real work.

  1. The contract comes first. The principal commits to a wage schedule before the agent acts. She cannot change it once she sees output. We will see in Interpreting the second-best contract that this commitment matters: in equilibrium the principal will punish bad outcomes even though she knows the agent worked.

  2. The action comes after acceptance. The agent knows the contract when he chooses his action. So the principal can predict how he will respond to any contract she offers, and she designs the contract with that response in mind.

  3. Information is symmetric at signing. Nobody knows anything the other does not when the contract is signed. The asymmetry arises only afterwards, when the agent acts unseen. This is what distinguishes moral hazard from the hidden-information problems of Chapters 4 and 5.

Who has the bargaining power? The principal makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer, so she has all the bargaining power and the agent is held to his outside option. This is a simplification. If principals competed for agents, the agent would capture more of the surplus. The shape of the optimal contract, which is what we care about most, would not change: whoever gets the surplus, it is still cheapest to induce effort with the contract we derive below. The case studies show that the prediction about who gets the surplus is the one that fails most often in practice.

3.2.8. Equilibrium and the two-stage method#

Definition 3.2 (Equilibrium)

A (subgame-perfect) equilibrium is a contract \(w^*\) and an agent strategy such that:

  • (a) for every contract \(w\), the agent accepts if and only if his best expected payoff from accepting is at least \(\uo\), and if he accepts he chooses an action that maximises \(\E[u(w(y))\mid a]-c(a)\);

  • (b) given the agent’s strategy, \(w^*\) maximises the principal’s expected payoff.

Tie-breaking. When indifferent, the agent accepts, and takes the action the principal recommends.

Remark 3.1 (Why the tie-breaking rule is harmless)

If the agent strictly preferred to reject, the principal could add an arbitrarily small \(\varepsilon\) to every \(z_i\) and he would accept. The rule just lets us write the limit of such contracts as an optimum rather than an infimum. The same is true for the action choice.

The equilibrium is found by backward induction, and in this model backward induction takes a particularly useful form, due to Grossman and Hart (1983). Rather than ask “what is the best contract?” in one go, we split the principal’s problem into two stages.

Proof toolkit: the two-stage method

  • Stage 1: implementation. For each action \(a\), find the cheapest contract that implements \(a\): one that the agent accepts and under which he chooses \(a\). Call its expected cost \(C(a)\).

  • Stage 2: selection. Choose the action that maximises expected output minus \(C(a)\).

Stage 1 is a cost-minimisation problem, like a firm finding the cheapest way to produce a given quantity. Stage 2 compares benefits with costs. Every theorem in this chapter has this structure. Hidden action changes only Stage 1: it makes some actions more expensive to implement.

The constraints in Stage 1 have names that recur throughout contract theory:

  • the participation constraint (PC) says the agent prefers accepting the contract to his outside option;

  • the incentive-compatibility constraint (IC) says the agent prefers the action the principal wants to any other action.

When the action is contractible, only (PC) is needed. When it is hidden, (IC) must be added. The whole chapter is about the cost of that one extra constraint.

3.2.9. The three ingredients of moral hazard#

Hidden action creates a real problem only when three ingredients are present at once. Remove any one of them and the principal can achieve the first best.

3.2.10. A running example#

We use one set of numbers throughout the chapter. It is based on the NHS case in The model meets the data: two cases. Money is in thousands of pounds a year.

Example 3.1 (The GP practice)

A GP looks after the patients on his practice’s list who have long-term conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure. In a good year most of them have their condition under control, and the health they gain is worth 400 to the NHS; in a bad year it is worth 200. (At £20,000 per quality-adjusted life year, the lower end of the range NICE has traditionally used, that is 20 and 10 QALYs.) So \(y_g=400\) and \(y_b=200\). Proactive care, calling patients in for reviews, adjusting their medicines and chasing those who miss appointments, raises the chance of a good year from \(\mul=0.35\) to \(\muh=0.65\). His utility of money is \(u(w)=\sqrt w\), so \(h(z)=z^2\). His outside option is salaried or locum work paying 100 a year, so \(\uo=\sqrt{100}=10\). Proactive care costs him \(c=0.3\) utils in time and effort.

  • The high action raises the expected health gain by \(\Dpi\Dy=0.3\times200=60\) a year.

  • In money, the effort cost is worth \(h(\uo+c)-h(\uo)=10.3^2-100 =6.09\) to him at his outside option: about £6,000 a year.

  • Effort is clearly worth buying if it can be bought: it produces 60 and costs 6.09. The question is what it costs when it cannot be observed.
