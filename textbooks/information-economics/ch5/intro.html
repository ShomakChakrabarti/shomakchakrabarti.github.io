5.1. Introduction#

5.1.1. A degree, a warranty and a dividend#

Two graduates apply for the same job. One has a first-class degree, the other a 2:2. The employer will learn within a year or two which of them is better at the job, but has to decide now, and the degree class is one of the few things she can see. Even if nothing studied for the degree is used in the job, the employer may reasonably prefer the first: getting a First is harder for someone who is less able, so the First says something about the person who earned it.

A car dealer offers a three-year warranty on the cars he sells. A warranty costs a dealer little if his cars are sound and a great deal if they are not. So a dealer who knows his cars are unreliable cannot afford to copy the offer, and buyers can read the warranty as a statement about quality that is backed by money.

A company raises its dividend. Paying out cash is costly for a firm that needs it, and a firm whose prospects are poor would soon have to cut the dividend again. Investors often read a dividend increase as good news, and share prices tend to rise when one is announced.

In each case:

  • one party (the graduate, the dealer, the firm) knows something about its own quality that the other party does not;

  • it takes a visible, costly action before the other side decides;

  • the action is cheaper for high-quality types than for low-quality ones, and that is why the other side can learn from it.

5.1.2. What “signalling” means#

Chapter 4 showed that hidden information can destroy a market, because the informed side cannot credibly tell the uninformed side what it knows: words are free, so everyone says the same thing. Michael Spence (1973) asked what happens if the informed side can do something rather than say something. His answer is that an action can carry information if it is more costly for some types than for others, and he showed this with a model of education in the labour market. Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize with Akerlof and Stiglitz.

Definition 5.1 (Signalling)

Signalling is a costly, observable action taken by an informed party before trade, whose cost depends on the private information, so that the uninformed party can draw inferences from it.

In this chapter the high type never wants to leave the market: her outside option does not depend on her type. The inefficiency is different. Resources are spent on an action that, in the basic model, produces nothing and exists only to be seen.

5.1.4. The central mechanism: costly to fake#

Why would an employer believe a degree says anything? Because a low-ability worker could get the degree, but it would cost her more than it is worth. The heart of the chapter is this no-mimicking condition. A signal separates the types only if it is expensive enough that the low type would rather be recognised as low than pay to look high, and cheap enough for the high type that she still finds it worth paying. Both conditions can hold only if the signal is cheaper for the high type. This is the single-crossing condition, and it does in this chapter the work that the outside option did in Chapter 4.

5.1.5. What you will learn#

After working through this chapter you should be able to:

  1. set up the Spence model as a game of incomplete information and define a perfect Bayesian equilibrium for it;

  2. solve the benchmarks with public productivity and with hidden productivity and no signal;

  3. characterise all separating equilibria, and explain why there are many and which is least costly;

  4. characterise all pooling equilibria, and explain the role of beliefs off the equilibrium path;

  5. compare welfare across equilibria and explain why signalling can make everyone worse off;

  6. map the model onto evidence on credentials and on degree requirements, and judge which predictions hold;

  7. (Advanced) apply the intuitive criterion, solve for signalling with a continuum of types, and analyse verifiable disclosure.

What you need. Bayes’ rule and expected values from Chapter 2; the idea of Bertrand competition; the notion of a best response.

Roadmap. The model sets out the model. Benchmarks solves two benchmarks. Separating equilibria and Pooling equilibria characterise separating and pooling equilibria. Welfare compares welfare. The model meets the data takes the predictions to evidence on credentials and degree inflation. Generalisations generalises for research students. Exercises and corrections close the chapter.

Proof toolkit: four techniques used throughout this chapter

  • (T1) Bayes’ rule on and off the path. After an action that some type takes with positive probability, beliefs follow from Bayes’ rule. After an action no type takes, Bayes’ rule says nothing, and perfect Bayesian equilibrium allows any belief. Choosing those off-path beliefs is how equilibria are constructed.

  • (T2) Bertrand undercutting. When two firms compete for a worker, neither can pay less than the worker’s expected productivity (the rival would outbid) or more (it would lose money). So the wage equals expected productivity given the firms’ beliefs.

  • (T3) Incentive (no-mimicking) constraints. An equilibrium in which types take different actions must leave each type unwilling to take the other’s action. Writing these two inequalities down is the whole of the separating analysis.

  • (T4) Single crossing. If an increase in the signal costs the high type less than the low type, then any signal level the high type is just willing to pay for is one the low type is unwilling to pay for. This turns two inequalities into an interval of solutions.
