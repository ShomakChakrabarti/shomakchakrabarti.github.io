5.7.1. Case 1: same skills, different certificate#

Graduates earn more than non-graduates. That fits Spence, but it also fits the view that education makes people more productive. To isolate the signal we need two workers with the same skills, one holding the certificate and one not. Three studies find such pairs.

The GED. The General Educational Development credential is the second-chance certificate for Americans who leave school without a diploma: five tests taking \(7\tfrac34\) hours, with about half a million passes in 1996. Each state sets its own pass mark, so a dropout with a given score passes in one state and fails in another. Tyler, Murnane and Willett (2000) compare such dropouts, aged 16–21, five years later: among young white dropouts, holding the GED raised earnings by 10–19%, about $1,500 a year. They find no effect for minority dropouts.

Degree class at LSE. A First needs enough course marks at 70, a 2:1 enough at 60, and the decisive mark (the fourth-best) is marked anonymously. Feng and Graetz (2017) compare 2,649 graduates of 2005–10 just above and just below each threshold, six months after graduating. A First raises the chance of working in a high-wage industry by 13 points and expected wages by about 3%; a 2:1 (against a 2:2) by 8 points and about 7%. The effects are larger for men and on mathematical programmes, where ability varies more.

The Texas diploma, and two limits. Clark and Martorell (2014) study 37,571 Texas students at their last-chance exit exam. Just passing raises the share holding a diploma from 40% to 90%, but its effect on earnings over eleven years is close to zero: employers learn about workers from interviews, tests and trial periods. Lange (2007) estimates that employers’ initial errors about a worker halve within three years, and that signalling accounts for under a quarter of the return to schooling. Arteaga (2018) finds that when a Colombian university cut its coursework by 20%, with the same intake, its graduates’ wages fell by about 16%: the coursework was productive.

Example 5.2 (Case 1 in numbers)

In Example 5.1, the least-cost separating signal solves \(2a^2=18\), so \(a^*=3\) years, costing the high type \(9\). The low type’s cost of three years is exactly the wage gain, \(18\), so she does not copy. The high type ends on \(38-9=29\), what she had with no signal; the low type falls from \(29\) to \(20\); total surplus falls from \(29\) to \(24.5\). Give a low type the certificate and she is paid \(38\), her twin without it \(20\): that is the GED comparison.

5.7.2. Case 2: degree inflation#

Fuller and Raman (2017), using 26 million job postings and a survey of 600 employers, found that 67% of US postings for production supervisors asked for a degree, while only 16% of people already doing the job had one. They estimated that more than 6 million jobs were at risk of “degree inflation”. In many middle-skill roles graduates were paid 11–30% more, although employers said experienced non-graduates performed “nearly or equally well”; graduates in these roles had higher turnover and lower engagement.

In 2022 many large employers announced they were dropping degree requirements. A 2024 follow-up by the Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School looked at who was actually hired by large firms over 2014–23. Only 3.6% of roles had dropped the requirement, adding about 97,000 non-graduate hires a year out of 77 million: “not even 1 in 700” hires changed. Of the firms that dropped it, 37% changed whom they hired, 45% changed nothing, and 18% went back. In England, the share of graduates awarded a First rose from 15.7% in 2010–11 to 37.9% in 2020–21, of which 22.4 percentage points could not be explained by changes in the students (Office for Students 2022).
