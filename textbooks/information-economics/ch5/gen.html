5.8. Generalisations#

Advanced: for research students

This section asks which off-path beliefs are reasonable and shows that a standard refinement selects the least-cost separating equilibrium; solves the model with a continuum of types; and contrasts costly signalling with verifiable disclosure.

5.8.1. Refining beliefs: the intuitive criterion#

Theorem 5.1 and Theorem 5.2 produce a continuum of equilibria, all supported by pessimistic beliefs off the path. Cho and Kreps (1987) proposed a test of whether such beliefs are reasonable. Let \(U^*_\theta\) be type \(\theta\)’s equilibrium payoff.

Definition 5.3 (Intuitive criterion)

Consider an off-path signal \(a\). Type \(\theta\) is equilibrium-dominated at \(a\) if even the best possible wage, \(\theta_h\), would leave it worse off than in equilibrium: \(\theta_h-c(a;\theta)<U^*_\theta\). A PBE fails the intuitive criterion if there is an off-path \(a\) at which \(\theta_l\) is equilibrium-dominated and \(\theta_h\) is not, and at which the high type would strictly gain if firms paid \(\theta_h\): \(\theta_h-c(a;\theta_h)>U^*_{\theta_h}\).

Interpretation. If a low type could never gain from sending \(a\), firms who see \(a\) should conclude it came from a high type, and pay accordingly. A high type who anticipates this has a speech in mind: “I am sending \(a\), which a low type would never send whatever you believed; so believe I am high.”

Proposition 5.3 (Selection of the least-cost separating equilibrium)

Under Assumption 5.1 and Assumption 5.2, the only PBE outcome that passes the intuitive criterion is the least-cost separating equilibrium, \(a_l^*=0\) and \(a_h^*=\underline a\).

Proof of Proposition 5.3

Step 1: separating with \(a_h^*>\underline a\) fails. Here \(U^*_{\theta_l}=\theta_l\) and \(U^*_{\theta_h}=\theta_h-c(a_h^*;\theta_h)\). Take \(a\in(\underline a,a_h^*)\). Then \(\theta_h-c(a;\theta_l)<\theta_h-c(\underline a;\theta_l)=\theta_l\): the low type is equilibrium-dominated. And \(\theta_h-c(a;\theta_h)>\theta_h-c(a_h^*;\theta_h)\): the high type gains.

Step 2: pooling at \(a_p\) fails. Here \(U^*_\theta=\bar\theta-c(a_p;\theta)\). We need \(a>a_p\) with

\[ c(a;\theta_l)-c(a_p;\theta_l)>\theta_h-\bar\theta>c(a;\theta_h)-c(a_p;\theta_h). \]

Let \(a'\) solve \(c(a';\theta_l)-c(a_p;\theta_l)=\theta_h-\bar\theta\) (it exists by continuity and unboundedness). By single crossing, \(c(a';\theta_h)-c(a_p;\theta_h)<\theta_h-\bar\theta\), and by continuity the same strict inequality holds for \(a\) slightly above \(a'\), while the first inequality holds strictly for all \(a>a'\).

Step 3: the least-cost separating equilibrium passes. Off-path signals are \(a\notin\{0,\underline a\}\). For \(a<\underline a\) the low type is not equilibrium-dominated (\(\theta_h-c(a;\theta_l)>\theta_l\)), so the criterion places no restriction. For \(a>\underline a\) the high type cannot gain, since \(\theta_h-c(a;\theta_h)<\theta_h-c(\underline a;\theta_h)\). So no signal breaks it.

Why this step?

The criterion works by ruling out implausible pessimism: beliefs that treat as a low type a signal that only a high type could want to send. Those are exactly the beliefs that sustain wasteful separation and pooling.

In Example 5.1, pooling at \(a_p\approx2.12\) is broken by any \(a\in(3,3.67)\) (Exercise Exercise 5.14). The intuitive criterion is not the last word: with \(\mu_0\) large, the pooling outcome at \(0\) gives the high type more than separation, and other refinements (e.g. Mailath, Okuno-Fujiwara and Postlewaite 1993) can select it.

5.8.2. A continuum of types#

Let \(\theta\) be distributed on \([\underline\theta,\bar\theta]\) with \(\underline\theta>0\), and \(c(a;\theta)=a/\theta\). Look for a separating equilibrium in which \(a(\theta)\) is strictly increasing and differentiable, so firms infer \(\theta\) from \(a\) and pay \(w(a)=a^{-1}(a)\), the inverse function.

Proposition 5.4 (Separating signals with a continuum of types)

The separating equilibrium in which the lowest type does not signal has

\[ a(\theta)=\frac{\theta^2-\underline\theta^2}{2}. \]

Type \(\theta\)’s payoff is \(\theta-a(\theta)/\theta=\tfrac\theta2+\tfrac{\underline\theta^2}{2\theta}\).

Proof of Proposition 5.4

Type \(\theta\) chooses \(a\) to maximise \(\hat\theta(a)-a/\theta\), where \(\hat\theta=a^{-1}\). The first-order condition is \(\hat\theta'(a)=1/\theta\). In equilibrium \(\hat\theta(a(\theta))=\theta\), so \(\hat\theta'(a(\theta))=1/a'(\theta)\), giving \(a'(\theta)=\theta\). With \(a(\underline\theta)=0\) (the lowest type is identified anyway, so it does not pay to signal) this integrates to the formula. Single crossing (\(\partial^2c/\partial a\,\partial\theta=-1/\theta^2<0\)) ensures the first-order condition is sufficient; see Mailath (1987).

Why this step?

The two-type model gives a step; with a continuum every type must be deterred from mimicking the type just above it, which gives a differential equation instead of an inequality.

5.8.3. Verifiable disclosure#

Signalling is needed when the informed party cannot simply show what it knows. If it can, at no cost, the outcome is very different.

Proposition 5.5 (Unravelling of disclosure; Grossman 1981, Milgrom 1981)

Suppose a seller’s quality takes finitely many values \(\theta_1<\dots<\theta_n\), each with positive probability, and is known to her. She can either disclose it verifiably at no cost or stay silent, and buyers pay their expected value of quality. Then in every equilibrium the price equals the seller’s true quality: at most one type stays silent, and silence reveals which one.

Proof of Proposition 5.5

Let \(S\) be the set of types that stay silent. If \(S\) contained two or more types, the price after silence would be their average, strictly below the highest type in \(S\); that type would get more by disclosing. So \(S\) has at most one element, and if it has one, Bayes’ rule sets the price after silence equal to that type’s quality.

Why this step?

The argument is a downward induction: the best type always discloses, then the best of the rest, and so on. Silence cannot hide anyone, because it is read as “the best type that has not disclosed”, and that type would rather disclose.

This is the opposite of Chapter 4: there, sellers could not prove quality and the market unravelled downwards; here they can, and silence unravels upwards, so “no news is bad news”. Signalling sits between the two: information is revealed, but at a cost.

Further reading. Spence (1973, 1974); Riley (1979); Cho and Kreps (1987); Mailath (1987); Mas-Colell, Whinston and Green (1995, ch. 13); Bolton and Dewatripont (2005, ch. 3).
