5.2. The model#

5.2.1. The model in one paragraph#

A worker is of high or low productivity, and only she knows which. Before looking for a job she chooses how much education to acquire. Education is costly, more so for the low type, and does not change productivity. Two firms see the education level, not productivity, and compete for the worker by offering wages. The worker takes the better offer. What education levels and wages arise in equilibrium, and is anyone better off because education can be observed?

5.2.2. Players and types#

There is one worker and two identical firms. The worker’s productivity is \(\theta\in\{\theta_l,\theta_h\}\) with \(\theta_h>\theta_l>0\); we write \(\Delta\coloneqq\theta_h-\theta_l\). The worker knows \(\theta\); the firms do not. Their common prior is \(\mu_0\coloneqq\Pr(\theta=\theta_h)\in(0,1)\), and we write \(\bar\theta\coloneqq\mu_0\theta_h+(1-\mu_0)\theta_l\) for expected productivity.

Interpretation. Productivity is the value of the worker’s output to a firm. “Two types” is the simplest way to model a characteristic that the worker knows better than the firm; Generalisations allows a continuum.

5.2.3. The signal#

Before the job market the worker chooses a signal \(a\ge0\), such as years of education. It costs her \(c(a;\theta)\).

Assumption 5.1 (Signal cost)

For each \(\theta\), \(c(\cdot\,;\theta)\) is continuous and strictly increasing, with \(c(0;\theta)=0\) and \(c(a;\theta)\to\infty\) as \(a\to\infty\).

Assumption 5.2 (Single crossing; Spence–Mirrlees)

For all \(a>a'\ge0\),

\[ c(a;\theta_l)-c(a';\theta_l)\ >\ c(a;\theta_h)-c(a';\theta_h). \]

Any increase in the signal costs the low type strictly more than the high type. In particular (take \(a'=0\)), \(c(a;\theta_l)>c(a;\theta_h)\) for \(a>0\).

Assumption 5.3 (Unproductive signal)

The signal does not affect productivity: a type-\(\theta\) worker produces \(\theta\) whatever \(a\) she chose.

Interpretation. With differentiable costs, single crossing says \(\partial c/\partial a\) is lower for the high type: the marginal cost of a further year of study is lower for the able. It is a statement about differences, which is what matters for incentives. The name comes from the picture: in the plane of signal and wage, an indifference curve of the low type and one of the high type cross only once (Fig. 5.1). Assuming the signal is unproductive isolates its informational role; real education is also productive, and The model meets the data shows that this matters.

5.2.4. Payoffs#

Assumption 5.4 (Payoffs)

Everyone is risk neutral. A type-\(\theta\) worker who chooses \(a\) and accepts wage \(w\) gets \(u(w,a;\theta)=w-c(a;\theta)\). A firm that hires a type-\(\theta\) worker at wage \(w\) gets \(\theta-w\). A firm that does not hire gets \(0\); a worker who rejects both offers gets \(-c(a;\theta)\) (her outside option is \(0\) before signalling costs).

Interpretation. The outside option does not depend on \(\theta\): this is the key difference from Chapter 4. Both firms are risk neutral, so they care only about expected productivity. Competition between two firms will push wages up to expected productivity; with one firm the worker would be paid her outside option and the signal would be pointless.

5.2.5. Timing#

5.2.6. Strategies, beliefs and equilibrium#

A strategy for the worker is a signal for each type, \(a(\theta)\), and a rule for accepting offers. A strategy for firm \(i\) is a wage schedule \(w_i(a)\): a wage offer for every signal it might observe. A belief system \(\mu(a)\coloneqq\mu(\theta_h\mid a)\) gives the probability firms assign to the high type after seeing \(a\).

Definition 5.2 (Perfect Bayesian equilibrium)

A perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE) consists of signals \(a^*(\theta)\), wage schedules \(w_1^*(a),w_2^*(a)\), acceptance decisions and beliefs \(\mu^*(a)\) such that:

  • (a) each type chooses \(a^*(\theta)\) to maximise \(\max_iw^*_i(a)-c(a;\theta)\), given the wage schedules;

  • (b) after each \(a\), each firm’s offer maximises its expected profit given the belief \(\mu^*(a)\) and the other firm’s offer;

  • (c) after each \(a\) and pair of offers, the worker accepts the higher one (if they are equal, each firm with probability \(\tfrac12\));

  • (d) beliefs follow Bayes’ rule whenever \(a\) is chosen by some type with positive probability; after other signals they are arbitrary.

An equilibrium is separating if \(a^*(\theta_h)\ne a^*(\theta_l)\) and pooling if \(a^*(\theta_h)=a^*(\theta_l)\).

Remark 5.1 (Why off-path beliefs matter)

Part (d) gives the model its flexibility. An action nobody takes is never observed, so the equilibrium can specify what firms would believe if they saw it. Those beliefs determine what a deviation would earn, and so which actions can be sustained. Much of this chapter is about what such beliefs can be, and Generalisations asks which ones are reasonable.

5.2.8. A running example#

Example 5.1 (Graduates)

Money is in thousands of pounds a year. Let \(\theta_l=20\), \(\theta_h=38\), so \(\Delta=18\), and \(\mu_0=\tfrac12\), so \(\bar\theta=29\). The signal is years of higher education, with costs

\[ c(a;\theta_l)=2a^2,\qquad c(a;\theta_h)=a^2 . \]

Single crossing holds: \(2(a^2-a'^2)>a^2-a'^2\) whenever \(a>a'\). We will find that three years of education separate the types; that the high type is then no better off than with no signal at all; and that both types can be stuck paying for a degree that tells firms nothing.
