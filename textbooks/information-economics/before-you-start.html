Before you start: why an AI summary will not teach you this#

The rule

You may not upload, paste, photograph or screenshot this textbook, the lecture slides or the tutorial materials into ChatGPT or any other AI tool, including to get a summary. This is a course rule for ECON20512, and it applies to every chapter, slide and worksheet.

The only exception is a task whose brief explicitly asks you to use an AI tool; that brief says what you may give the tool.

Why the rule exists#

It is tempting to paste a chapter into ChatGPT, ask for a summary and learn the summary. It feels efficient, and for some subjects it might be. For this course it does not work, for three reasons.

This course is about arguments, not facts. Almost nothing in this book is a fact to be remembered. It is a sequence of arguments: why the incentive constraint binds, why the high type would rather pay for education than be pooled, why the market for used cars can unravel. A summary keeps the conclusions and throws away the arguments. In the examination you will be given a situation you have not seen and asked to build the argument yourself. Conclusions you have memorised will not get you there.

Reading a summary feels like understanding when it is not. A clear summary is easy to read, and easy reading feels like learning. Psychologists call this the illusion of fluency. The research on how people learn points the other way: you remember and understand material that you have had to work at, by trying to recall it, attempting a proof before reading it, or predicting a result before checking it. (A well-known demonstration is Roediger and Karpicke, 2006, on the testing effect.) A summary removes exactly the effort that does the learning.

AI summaries of this material are often wrong in the places that matter. They are reliable on the headline and unreliable on the subtle points, which are the points the examination tests. Typical errors include:

  • saying that in the second-best contract only the incentive constraint binds, when the participation constraint binds too;

  • saying that education in the signalling model makes workers more productive, when in the basic model it does not, and the point is that it is costly in a way that differs by type;

  • saying that adverse selection always makes the market collapse, when the book shows when it collapses completely, when only partly, and what prevents it.

You would have no way of telling which parts of a summary were wrong, because you would not have read the original.

There is also a simpler reason. These materials are written for, and licensed to, students enrolled on this course. Uploading them to an AI service passes them to a third party.

What to do instead#

  1. Read a section, then close it. Write down the main argument from memory in three or four lines. Then check it against the text. Where you could not reconstruct a step, that is what to reread.

  2. Try the proof before you open it. Every proof is folded away. Spend two minutes on how you would prove the result, and open the Proof strategy box first if there is one.

  3. Predict before you check. Before you move a slider in an interactive lab or read a result, commit to what you think will happen.

  4. Use the exercises. The Core exercises at the end of each chapter are the quickest test of whether you have understood it.

  5. Bring what you cannot do to tutorials and office hours. That is what they are for.

What this site does#

So that the materials stay where they belong:

  • the book and slides are read online and are not offered as downloads;

  • printing, including Save as PDF, produces a notice instead of the page;

  • text cannot be selected or copied;

  • pages carry a faint watermark.

Screen readers, magnifiers and keyboard navigation still work. If you need a printable or downloadable copy, or anything else, as a reasonable adjustment, contact the course team and it will be provided.
