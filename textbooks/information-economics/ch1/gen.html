1.7. Generalisations#

Advanced: for research students

This section extends the model to many periods, proves that a firm’s investment decision can be separated from its owners’ tastes, and notes what changes when borrowing is limited.

1.7.1. Many periods#

With income \(x_0,\dots,x_T\) and utility \(\sum_{t=0}^T\delta^tu(c_t)\), the budget becomes \(\sum_t c_t/(1+r)^t=\sum_t x_t/(1+r)^t\), and the same argument as in Theorem 1.1 gives the Euler equation between any two adjacent periods: \(u'(c_t)=\delta(1+r)\,u'(c_{t+1})\). With \(u=\ln c\), \(c_t=\big(\delta(1+r)\big)^tc_0\): consumption grows if and only if \(\delta(1+r)>1\), whatever the timing of income. This is the core of the permanent income hypothesis.

1.7.2. Fisher separation#

Theorem 1.3 (Fisher separation)

A consumer with income \((x_0,x_1)\) can invest \(k\in[0,x_0]\) today in a technology that returns \(f(k)\) tomorrow, with \(f\) increasing and strictly concave, and can borrow and lend at rate \(r\). Then her optimal investment maximises \(-k+f(k)/(1+r)\), the project’s NPV, whatever her preferences.

Proof of Theorem 1.3

Step 1: the budget. Any investment \(k\) followed by borrowing or lending gives exactly the plans with \(c_0+c_1/(1+r)=x_0+x_1/(1+r)+\big[-k+f(k)/(1+r)\big]\).

Step 2: more wealth is better. Raising the bracket shifts the budget line outwards in parallel, enlarging the budget set. Since \(U\) is strictly increasing, the best plan on a larger set is at least as good. So she should choose \(k\) to maximise the bracket, and then choose consumption on the resulting line.

Why this step?

The capital market lets her undo any timing of the project’s returns, so only their present value matters.

Interpretation. The owners of a firm can disagree about how patient to be and still agree on what the firm should do: maximise NPV. This is why managers can be told to maximise value. It fails when the owners cannot borrow and lend at the same rate, and, as What the model assumes about the future suggests, it fails in its simple form once returns are risky.

1.7.3. Borrowing constraints#

If the consumer cannot borrow (\(s_0\ge0\)), the budget line stops at the endowment. A would-be borrower (Proposition 1.1) then consumes her income, and her Euler equation holds as an inequality, \(u'(x_0)>\delta(1+r)u'(x_1)\). Students, young households and small firms are the usual examples. Why lenders limit borrowing, rather than raising the rate, is a question about hidden information that Chapter 4 answers.
