4.1. Introduction#

4.1.1. A used car, a health plan and a loan#

You are buying a second-hand car. The owner tells you it runs perfectly and has had one careful owner. It may well be true. But the owner has driven the car for years and knows whether the gearbox slips when it is cold; you have had it for a ten-minute test drive. You also know that an owner whose car runs perfectly has a good reason to keep it, and an owner whose gearbox is about to fail has a good reason to sell. So the very fact that this car is for sale is a little bit of bad news about it.

In 1995 Harvard University changed how it subsidised its employees’ health insurance. Employees who chose the generous plan suddenly had to pay much more for it. The employees who left that plan were, on average, younger and healthier than those who stayed. The plan’s costs per member rose, its premium rose, more of the healthy left, and within three years the plan was closed.

A bank that lends at a high interest rate attracts the borrowers who are most willing to pay it: often those whose projects are riskiest, because they do not expect to repay if things go badly. Raising the interest rate can then lower the bank’s expected return.

These three situations share a structure.

  • A good or a contract is traded between two parties.

  • One party knows something about its quality that the other does not: the condition of the car, the employee’s health, the riskiness of the project.

  • The informed party’s decision to trade depends on that quality. Owners of good cars are less willing to sell; healthy employees are less willing to pay for generous insurance; safe borrowers are less willing to borrow at high rates.

  • So the uninformed party faces a selected sample: the units actually on offer are worse, on average, than the units that exist.

4.1.2. What “adverse selection” means#

The name comes from insurance, where it describes the tendency of insurance to be bought disproportionately by those most likely to claim. George Akerlof’s paper “The Market for ‘Lemons’ ” (1970) showed that the same force operates in any market where sellers know more than buyers about quality, and that it can be strong enough to destroy the market altogether. In American slang a “lemon” is a car that turns out to be defective. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Michael Spence, whose work on signalling is the subject of Chapter 5, and Joseph Stiglitz, whose work on screening and credit rationing developed the lending example above.

Definition 4.1 (Adverse selection)

Adverse selection is a situation in which one party to a trade has private information about the quality of what is traded, before the trade, and the decision to trade depends on that information, so that the units offered are systematically worse (for the uninformed party) than the population of units.

4.1.4. The central mechanism: the price changes what is for sale#

In an ordinary market, the price tells buyers and sellers how scarce a good is, and the good itself is the same whatever the price. Under adverse selection, the price also changes which units are offered. A lower price drives out the sellers with the best units, because their outside option (keeping the good) is worth more. Buyers understand this, so a lower price makes them value the average unit on offer less, which pushes the price down further. This feedback loop, between the price and the quality of what is for sale, is the heart of the chapter. In the model below it runs all the way down: the market trades nothing but the worst unit. The rest of the chapter asks when that happens, what stops it, and what the evidence says.

4.1.5. What you will learn#

After working through this chapter you should be able to:

  1. set up a market with hidden quality and compute buyers’ willingness to pay as a conditional expectation;

  2. solve for equilibrium when quality is public, when nobody knows it, and when only sellers know it, and prove each result;

  3. explain why the market can unravel completely, and identify exactly which assumption drives this;

  4. analyse the two remedies the model suggests, equalising outside options and verifying quality, and compute their effect;

  5. map the model onto used cars and health insurance and judge which predictions hold in data;

  6. (Advanced) characterise equilibria with general distributions, explain multiplicity, and use the demand–cost-curve framework for insurance markets.

What you need. Expected values and conditional expectations, the uniform distribution, and Bayes’ rule from Chapter 2. The advanced section uses a little real analysis (monotone functions, fixed points).

Roadmap. The model sets out the model and explains every assumption. Benchmarks: symmetric information solves two benchmarks in which information is symmetric. Asymmetric information: the market for lemons proves Akerlof’s result for asymmetric information and interprets the unravelling. Remedies studies remedies. Welfare compares welfare. The model meets the data: two cases takes the predictions to used cars and to Harvard’s health plan. Generalisations generalises the model for research students. Exercises and corrections close the chapter.

Proof toolkit: four techniques used throughout this chapter

  • (T1) Truncated expectations. If \(\theta\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), then \(\E[\theta\mid\theta\le p]=p/2\) and \(\E[\theta\mid a\le\theta\le p]=(a+p)/2\). More generally, \(\E[\theta\mid\theta\le c]\) is nondecreasing in \(c\). Buyers’ willingness to pay is always one of these.

  • (T2) The excess-demand argument. With more buyers than sellers, a price below what buyers would pay cannot be an equilibrium: some seller could ask a little more and still sell. This is a perturbation argument, like those of Chapter 3.

  • (T3) Fixed points. An equilibrium price satisfies \(p=\varphi(p)\), where \(\varphi(p)\) is what buyers will pay when the price is \(p\). Draw \(\varphi\) and the \(45^\circ\) line and look at where they meet.

  • (T4) Case analysis. When an argument divides by something or compares two curves, check every case. The lecture version of Akerlof’s theorem misses the case \(k\ge2\) for exactly this reason.
