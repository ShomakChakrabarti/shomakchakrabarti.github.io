4.5. Remedies#

The proof of Theorem 4.1 used two features of the market: the seller’s outside option rises with quality, and buyers cannot verify quality. Each suggests a remedy.

4.5.1. Equalising outside options#

Suppose every seller’s outside option is the same number \(r\in[0,1]\), whatever the quality of his unit. Think of a car owner who is emigrating and cannot take the car, a leasing company that must dispose of every car at the end of the lease, or, in insurance, an employee whose alternative to the generous plan is equally attractive whatever his health.

Proposition 4.5 (Constant outside option)

Suppose sellers observe \(\theta\), buyers do not, and every seller’s outside option is \(r\).

  • (a) If \(r\le k/2\), every unit trades at \(p^*=k/2\).

  • (b) If \(r>k/2\), nothing trades.

In either case the outcome is the one a planner who cannot observe \(\theta\) would choose: trade everything if the average unit is worth more to buyers than to sellers, and nothing otherwise.

Proof strategy (Proposition 4.5)

Follow the proof of Theorem 4.1 and watch Step 1 change: a seller’s decision no longer depends on \(\theta\), so the units offered are again a random sample, as in Proposition 4.2.

Proof of Proposition 4.5

Step 1: who sells. A seller sells at \(p\) if and only if \(p\ge r\), whatever his \(\theta\). So at any price either all units are offered or none.

Why this step?

This is the step that breaks the spiral. Because selling is equally attractive to all sellers, the decision to sell reveals nothing about quality.

Step 2: what buyers will pay. When all units are offered, buyers’ expected value is \(k\E[\theta]=k/2\).

Step 3: the equilibrium. The acceptable prices are \([r,k/2]\). If \(r\le k/2\) this is non-empty and its maximum is \(k/2\): every seller sells and every buyer is willing to buy. If \(r>k/2\) it is empty and nothing trades.

Step 4: the planner’s benchmark. A planner who cannot see \(\theta\) must treat all units alike. Moving all units to buyers generates \(\int_0^1(k\theta-r)\,d\theta=k/2-r\), which is positive exactly when \(r<k/2\). The market makes the same choice.

Interpretation. When the decision to sell is unrelated to quality, there is no selection, and buyers face the population average. This is why forced sales (leases ending, owners moving abroad) are less affected by adverse selection, and why insurance mandates work: if everyone must buy, enrolling reveals nothing about health (The model meets the data: two cases). In health insurance, a strong public alternative such as the NHS can play a similar role, by making the outside option of not buying private cover much the same for the healthy and the sick.

A caveat on efficiency. The slides call this outcome efficient. It is efficient given what can be observed. Ex post, units with \(k\theta<r\) are worth more to their sellers than to their buyers, yet they are traded in case (a). A planner who could see \(\theta\) would keep them with their owners and achieve \(\int_{r/k}^1(k\theta-r)\,d\theta=(k-r)^2/(2k)\), which exceeds the market’s \(k/2-r\) by \(r^2/(2k)\). In the running example with \(r=0.3\) the gap is \(0.03\), against \(0.48\) achievable.

Policy. The lesson is that government intervention to equalise outside options can restore trade. But changing outside options is not always feasible or desirable: as the slides put it, the government cannot change how much I value my own car. Mandates and subsidies work in insurance because the “outside option” there is a policy choice.

4.5.2. Verifying quality#

The second remedy attacks the other ingredient: make some information about quality credible. Full verification brings us back to Proposition 4.1. But partial verification can already do a lot. The simplest form is a quality floor: an inspection certifies only that \(\theta\ge a\). This is what CarMax does by refusing to sell cars with flood damage, frame damage or a salvage history.

Proposition 4.6 (A quality floor)

Suppose \(1<k<2\) and only units with \(\theta\ge a\) are offered in the market, where \(a\in(0,1)\), and buyers know this. Then:

  • (a) if \(a<(2-k)/k\), the equilibrium price is \(p^*=\dfrac{ka}{2-k}\) and units with \(\theta\in[a,p^*]\) trade;

  • (b) if \(a\ge(2-k)/k\), every certified unit trades, at \(p^*=k(a+1)/2\).

Proof of Proposition 4.6

At price \(p\ge a\), the units offered are those with \(\theta\in[a,\min\{p,1\}]\), whose mean is \((a+\min\{p,1\})/2\) (T1). So \(\varphi(p)=k(a+\min\{p,1\})/2\).

For \(a\le p\le1\), \(\varphi(p)\ge p\) if and only if \(ka+kp\ge2p\), i.e. \(p\le ka/(2-k)\). Note \(ka/(2-k)\ge a\) because \(k\ge2-k\). If \(ka/(2-k)<1\), which is the condition \(a<(2-k)/k\), no \(p>1\) is acceptable either (since then \(\varphi(p)=k(a+1)/2<1<p\); check: \(k(a+1)/2<1\) is equivalent to \(a<(2-k)/k\)). So \(p^*=ka/(2-k)\).

If \(a\ge(2-k)/k\), every \(p\in[a,1]\) is acceptable, and for \(p>1\) the price is acceptable if and only if \(p\le k(a+1)/2\), which is at least \(1\). So \(p^*=k(a+1)/2\) and every unit with \(\theta\ge a\) is offered.

Interpretation. A floor does not tell buyers which certified unit is good and which is mediocre. It works because it removes the bottom of the distribution, which anchors the average. Without the floor, the average of the units offered at \(p\) is \(p/2\), always well below \(p\). With a floor at \(a\), it is \((a+p)/2\), which can be close to \(p\). In the running example (\(k=1.5\)), a floor at \(a=0.2\) supports a price of \(0.6\), and cars in \([0.2,0.6]\) trade, recovering \((0.5)(0.36-0.04)/2=0.08\), about a third of the efficient surplus of \(0.25\). A floor at \(a\ge1/3\) makes every certified car trade.

Other mechanisms. Many institutions do the same job: warranties and money-back guarantees (a seller of a bad car would not offer them), dealer reputation, independent inspections such as the MOT, vehicle history reports, and online listings that are legally enforceable descriptions. In each case, the mechanism makes a statement about quality credible. When the mechanism is a costly action chosen by the informed party, it is a signal, the subject of Chapter 5. When it is a menu of contracts offered by the uninformed party, it is screening.
