4.7.1. Case 1: used cars#

Used cars are Akerlof’s own example. The owner knows whether the gearbox is failing; the buyer does not. An owner of a good car will not sell at the price of an average one, so good cars are withdrawn, the average quality offered falls, and the price follows it down. Theorem 4.1 says the price falls to zero and only lemons trade. Yet used cars are bought and sold in enormous numbers. Either an assumption fails, or someone is doing the work of Verifying quality.

Are traded cars really worse? Peterson and Schneider (2014) treat a car as a bundle of components, some a buyer can inspect and some not. Using repair records from the US Consumer Expenditure Survey, they find adverse selection for transmissions, engines and air-conditioning, which a buyer cannot easily check, and none for body condition, which a buyer can see. Genesove (1993) studies dealer-to-dealer auctions: a dealer who sends most of its trade-ins to auction is not picking out the bad ones, so its cars should be better and fetch more. He finds only weak evidence that they do.

How the market protects itself: CarMax. Founded in 1993 in Richmond, Virginia, CarMax now has over 240 stores and sells more than 750,000 used cars a year (Wells and Luo, 2017). Every car it retails passes an inspection of more than 125 points. Cars with flood damage, frame damage or a salvage history are never retailed; they are sold to other dealers at CarMax’s own auctions. Prices are fixed, with no haggling. Cars come with a 30-day limited warranty and a 10-day money-back guarantee. CarMax also buys cars from the public, with a written offer valid for seven days.

The hardest case: eBay Motors. On eBay Motors a buyer bids on a car hundreds of miles away that he has never seen; about $12 billion of vehicles were traded in 2006. Lewis (2011) explains why this works. The listing is a legally enforceable description; the buyer sees the car before final payment, and eBay pursues misrepresentation. Across more than 50,000 listings, each extra photo is associated with a price about 1.75% higher ($175 on the average car). Tadelis and Zettelmeyer (2015) ran a field experiment at dealer auctions: randomly disclosing a car’s condition raised revenue, for bad news as well as good.

4.7.2. Case 2: Harvard’s health plan#

About 10,000 Harvard employees chose between a generous plan, a preferred provider organisation (PPO) chosen by about 20% of staff, and several cheaper health maintenance organisations (HMOs). Until 1994 Harvard’s subsidy rose with the cost of the plan, so the PPO cost an employee only $400–$500 a year more than an HMO. In 1995 Harvard switched to an equal contribution: it paid the same fixed amount towards any plan and the employee paid the rest. The aim was to make the plans compete, and premiums did fall, by 5–8% (Cutler and Reber, 1998).

Cutler and Zeckhauser (1998) describe this as an adverse selection death spiral that turned very fast, with a welfare loss of 2–4% of baseline spending. Two comparisons help. Massachusetts state employees, whose employer paid 85–90% of whichever premium they chose, kept their generous plan: its share fell only from 30% to 27% by 1997. And when Massachusetts mandated health insurance in 2006, premiums and average costs in the individual market fell; Hackmann, Kolstad and Kowalski (2015) estimate a welfare gain from reduced adverse selection of 4.1% per person, or $51.1 million a year.

  • A5. The plan loses money every year on the way down: the premium is set for last year’s members, and this year’s are sicker.

  • A6. The long-run share enrolled is \((2k-2)/(2k-1)\): \(1/3\) at \(k=1.25\), \(1/6\) at \(k=1.1\), and \(0\) as \(k\to1\), which is Theorem 4.1. At \(k=1.25\), 44% of the surplus is lost.

  • A7. If the employer pays 85% of any premium, the employee faces \(0.15P\) and 94% stay enrolled. With a mandate everyone is in, the premium is \(0.50\) and all surplus is realised: enrolling reveals nothing, so the spiral has nothing to feed on.

Unlike Theorem 4.1, the spiral here stops part-way. The reason is that the employee’s value of cover, \(k(1-\theta)\), and the plan’s cost, \(1-\theta\), fall together as health improves, so the healthy enrolees who leave are also the cheap ones. 4.7.3. What the cases teach#

  1. The mechanism is real in both markets. A3 and A4 hold wherever quality is hard to see: gearboxes and health alike.

  2. Whether the market dies depends on the remedies. Car sellers verify. Harvard removed the equal outside option and did not price on health.

  3. Theorem Theorem 4.1 is a statement about a force. Use it to ask what is holding a market together, and what would happen if that were taken away.
