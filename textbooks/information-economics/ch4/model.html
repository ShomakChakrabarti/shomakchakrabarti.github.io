4.2. The model#

4.2.1. The model in one paragraph#

A market has many buyers and many sellers of a good whose quality varies from unit to unit. Each seller owns one unit and knows its quality. Each buyer wants one unit and does not know the quality of any particular unit, only how quality is distributed. A buyer values a unit of quality \(\theta\) at \(k\theta\); its owner values keeping it at \(\theta\); since \(k>1\), every unit is worth more to a buyer than to its owner. There are more buyers than sellers. Trade happens at a market price. We ask what that price is, and which units are traded.

4.2.2. Players and the good#

There are many identical buyers and many sellers. Each seller owns exactly one unit of the good. Units differ in quality \(\theta\in[0,1]\), where \(0\) is the worst possible unit and \(1\) the best. Across sellers, quality is spread evenly:

Assumption 4.1 (Uniform quality)

The qualities of the units owned by sellers are uniformly distributed on \([0,1]\). Buyers know this distribution.

The uniform distribution is chosen because it makes the conditional expectations in this chapter easy to compute; Generalisations shows which results depend on it. Think of \(\theta\) as an index of everything about the unit that matters to its user: for a car, the state of its engine, gearbox and body; for an insurance contract, the health of the insured.

4.2.3. Information#

Each seller observes the quality of his own unit. Buyers do not observe the quality of any unit before buying it. A buyer who considers a unit therefore forms an expectation of its quality, using the uniform distribution and anything he can infer from the market, for example from the fact that the unit is being offered at a given price.

We compare three information structures:

  • (i) quality is public: everyone observes \(\theta\);

  • (ii) quality is unknown to all: nobody observes \(\theta\) before trade;

  • (iii) quality is private: sellers observe \(\theta\), buyers do not.

Cases (i) and (ii) are benchmarks with symmetric information. Case (iii) is adverse selection. Comparing them isolates the effect of the asymmetry, as opposed to the effect of how much is known in total.

4.2.4. Payoffs#

Assumption 4.2 (Payoffs)

Everyone is risk-neutral. If a unit of quality \(\theta\) is traded at price \(p\):

  • the buyer gets \(k\theta-p\), where \(k>1\) is a fixed parameter;

  • the seller gets \(p\).

If a buyer does not trade he gets \(0\). If a seller does not trade he keeps his unit, which is worth \(\theta\) to him.

Interpreting \(k\). The parameter \(k>1\) measures the gains from trade: a unit is worth \(k\) times as much to a buyer as to its current owner. A buyer may need a car more than the seller, who is upgrading or moving away; an insurer pools risks that an individual cannot. Large \(k\) means trade is very valuable; \(k\) close to \(1\) means the units are worth almost the same to both sides. We will see that whether the market survives adverse selection depends on how large \(k\) is.

Interpreting the seller’s outside option. The most important assumption in the chapter is that the seller’s outside option is \(\theta\): it rises with the quality of his unit. An owner of a good car can keep driving a good car; an owner of a bad one is keeping a bad one. This is what makes the decision to sell informative: at any price, the owners who sell are the ones whose units are worth least to themselves. In Equalising outside options we replace \(\theta\) by a constant and show that the whole problem disappears.

Why risk neutrality? Risk aversion is not the issue in this chapter, so we remove it. Buyers care only about the expected quality of what they buy. That isolates the selection effect: any loss we find comes from units not being traded, not from anyone bearing risk.

4.2.5. Demand, supply and prices#

Assumption 4.3 (Market size)

Each seller supplies at most one unit; each buyer demands at most one unit. For every quality level there are more buyers than sellers.

Buyers are on the long side of the market. Competition among them for the scarce units drives the price up to the most that buyers are willing to pay. That is why, in every equilibrium below, buyers end up with no surplus and sellers capture all the gains from trade. The assumption simplifies the analysis; reversing it would change who gets the surplus, not which units are traded.

A price is a function \(p:[0,1]\to\R_+\) that says what a unit of each quality sells for. When buyers can see quality, the price can differ across qualities. When they cannot, it cannot: two units that look identical to buyers must sell at the same price (Lemma 4.1).

4.2.6. Timing#

4.2.7. Efficiency#

Before defining equilibrium, it helps to know what a good outcome looks like. Since every unit is worth \(k\theta\) to a buyer and only \(\theta\) to its owner, moving a unit from a seller to a buyer creates surplus \((k-1)\theta\ge0\), strictly positive for every \(\theta>0\).

Definition 4.2 (Efficiency)

An outcome is efficient if every unit with \(\theta>0\) is traded. Total surplus is then

\[ W^{\FB}=\int_0^1 (k-1)\theta\,d\theta=\frac{k-1}{2}. \]

Remember that trade is voluntary: nobody can be forced to buy or sell. Efficiency therefore requires a price at which every seller is willing to sell and every buyer is willing to buy.

4.2.8. Equilibrium#

We use a simple notion of competitive equilibrium that captures the excess-demand logic above.

Definition 4.3 (Market equilibrium)

Fix an information structure and a set of units that buyers cannot tell apart (all units, or all units of a given quality if quality is public). At a candidate price \(p\):

  • (a) the units offered are those whose sellers weakly prefer selling at \(p\) to keeping the unit, given what the seller knows;

  • (b) \(p\) is acceptable to buyers if some unit is offered and a buyer’s expected value of a unit drawn from those offered is at least \(p\).

The equilibrium price is the highest price acceptable to buyers. If no price is acceptable, there is no trade.

Remark 4.1 (Why the highest acceptable price?)

Suppose units trade at an acceptable price \(p\), but some higher price \(\tilde p\) is also acceptable. Because buyers outnumber sellers, some buyers are not served at \(p\). A seller could ask \(\tilde p\): buyers who were not served would accept, since \(\tilde p\) is acceptable, and the seller would be strictly better off. So \(p\) cannot be an equilibrium. This is the argument in the lecture slides (T2), applied once and for all. In Generalisations we discuss what happens when buyers’ willingness to pay is not monotone in the price, which can create several candidate equilibria.

4.2.9. The three ingredients of adverse selection#

As with moral hazard in Chapter 3, the problem needs several ingredients at once. Remove any one and the market is efficient.

Ingredient

What happens without it

Where it is shown

One side knows more than the other

Quality public or unknown to all: every unit trades

Proposition 4.1 and Proposition 4.2

The informed side’s outside option rises with quality

Selling reveals nothing; the market pools all units

Proposition 4.5

No credible way to verify quality

Verified units trade at prices that reflect quality

Proposition 4.6, Chapter 5

There is a fourth, quantitative condition: gains from trade must be small relative to how much quality varies. In our model that is \(k<2\) (Proposition 4.3).

4.2.10. A running example#

Example 4.1 (Used cars)

Take \(k=1.5\): a car is worth 50% more to the buyer than to the current owner. Quality \(\theta\) is uniform on \([0,1]\).

  • Efficient surplus is \((k-1)/2=0.25\) per seller.

  • The average car is worth \(\E[\theta]=0.5\) to its owner and \(k\E[\theta]=0.75\) to a buyer.

  • If only cars of quality up to \(p\) are offered, the average car on offer has quality \(p/2\) and is worth \(0.75p\) to a buyer.

The last line already contains the whole story: whatever the price, buyers value the cars on offer at only three quarters of it.
