4.3. Benchmarks: symmetric information#

Before introducing the asymmetry, we solve two benchmarks in which buyers and sellers know the same thing. In the first, everyone knows the quality of every unit. In the second, nobody does. Comparing them with the asymmetric case shows that the problem is not a lack of information as such; it is that the information is held by one side only.

4.3.1. Quality is public#

Proposition 4.1 (Public quality)

Suppose \(\theta\) is observed by everyone. Then a unit of quality \(\theta\) sells at

\[ p^*(\theta)=k\theta . \]

Buyers are indifferent between buying and not (and buy); sellers of every quality \(\theta>0\) strictly prefer to sell. Every unit trades, so the outcome is efficient.

Proof strategy (Proposition 4.1)

Because quality is observed, each quality level forms its own little market. In the market for quality \(\theta\), find which prices sellers accept and which buyers accept; the equilibrium price is the highest price acceptable to buyers (T2).

Proof of Proposition 4.1

Fix \(\theta>0\) and consider units of that quality.

Step 1: who sells. A seller sells at price \(p\) if and only if \(p\ge\theta\), his outside option.

Step 2: who buys. A buyer buys if and only if \(k\theta-p\ge0\), that is \(p\le k\theta\).

Step 3: the equilibrium price. The set of acceptable prices is \([\theta,k\theta]\), which is non-empty because \(k>1\). Its highest element is \(k\theta\).

Why this step?

Competition among the more numerous buyers drives the price to the top of the acceptable range. At any lower price a seller could ask a little more and an unserved buyer would still accept.

Step 4: efficiency. At \(p=k\theta\) the buyer gets \(0\), equal to his outside option, and buys by the tie-breaking rule. The seller gets \(k\theta>\theta\), so he strictly prefers to sell. This holds for every \(\theta>0\), so every unit trades. (For \(\theta=0\) the price is \(0\) and there are no gains from trade to lose.)

Interpretation. The market splits into separate sub-markets, one for each quality, each with its own price. Prices fully reflect quality: a car of quality \(0.8\) sells for \(1.2\), one of quality \(0.2\) for \(0.3\). All of the surplus goes to sellers, because buyers are on the long side of the market. Note that this is not price discrimination: different qualities are different goods, so it is natural that they sell at different prices.

Welfare. Each unit generates \((k-1)\theta\), so total surplus is \(W^{\FB}=(k-1)/2\), all of it to sellers. In the running example, \(0.25\).

4.3.2. Nobody knows quality#

Now suppose that neither side observes quality before trade: a fault that neither the owner nor the buyer knows about, such as a battery about to fail, or the quality of grapes before the harvest.

Proposition 4.2 (Quality unknown to all)

Suppose nobody observes \(\theta\). Then every unit sells at the single price

\[ p^*=\frac k2 . \]

Buyers are indifferent and buy; every seller strictly prefers to sell. Every unit trades, so the outcome is efficient.

Proof strategy (Proposition 4.2)

The same three steps as before, except that both sides now act on expected quality. Since nobody knows \(\theta\), a seller’s decision cannot depend on it, so the units offered are a random sample of all units.

Proof of Proposition 4.2

Step 1: who sells. A seller does not know \(\theta\), so the expected value of keeping his unit is \(\E[\theta]=1/2\). He sells at \(p\) if and only if \(p\ge1/2\). Every seller faces the same comparison, so either all units are offered (\(p\ge1/2\)) or none are.

Step 2: who buys. If all units are offered, a buyer’s expected value of a unit is \(k\E[\theta]=k/2\). He buys if and only if \(p\le k/2\).

Step 3: the equilibrium price. The acceptable prices form \([1/2,k/2]\), non-empty because \(k>1\). The highest is \(k/2\).

Why this step?

The argument is the same as in Proposition 4.1. The lecture slides refer to “the proof of Proposition 2” at this point; the argument being reused is that of Proposition 1.

Step 4: efficiency. At \(k/2\) each buyer’s expected payoff is \(0\) and he buys; each seller gets \(k/2>1/2\) and sells. Every unit trades.

Interpretation. There is now a single market with a single price. Ex post, some buyers are lucky (they get \(\theta\) close to \(1\) for \(k/2\)) and some are unlucky, but ex ante nobody is fooled, because nobody knows more than anyone else. The seller’s decision to sell carries no information, because the seller knows nothing that the buyer does not.

Welfare. A unit of quality \(\theta\) generates \((k-1)\theta\) however it is priced, and every unit trades, so total surplus is again \((k-1)/2\). Sellers get \(k/2-1/2=(k-1)/2\) in expectation and buyers get \(0\).

  1. Symmetric information gives efficiency, whether the information is complete or absent. What matters for trade is not how much is known, but whether it is known by both sides.

  2. Prices follow what is observed. When quality is public, price varies with it; when it is unknown, it cannot.

  3. The price only divides the surplus. Total surplus depends on which units trade, which is the same in both benchmarks; the price decides who gets it.

The results also hold if both sides observe the same imperfect signal of quality: then they share the same conditional expectation, and the argument of Proposition 4.2 applies with \(\E[\theta\mid\text{signal}]\) in place of \(\E[\theta]\).
